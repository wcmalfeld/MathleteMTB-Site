## 2. Real-world MTB industry connection

This is inspired by the real custom frame protection industry.

Companies such as Unleazhed allow riders to personalize MTB protection kits with their own artwork, logos and text.

Their products include:
- Top tube protection.
- Down tube protection.

Their smaller custom kits also allow riders to create personalized protection designs using uploaded artwork. <Cite refs={["turn378817view2","turn269602view0","turn269602view1"]}/>

The student should experience the creative and mathematical aspects of this work.

## 3. Educational philosophy

The central idea is:

**Polynomials describe the amount of material used in a design. Multiplication expands the design into its individual areas. Factoring reverses that process to recover the original dimensions and common patterns.**

This is particularly effective because it gives students a visual reason to understand algebraic expressions.

The student should not feel like they are simply manipulating x's and y's.

They should understand that:
- Adding polynomials combines different areas of material.
- Subtracting polynomials calculates remaining material.
- Multiplying polynomials expands rectangular dimensions into smaller areas.
- Factoring reverses multiplication and identifies the dimensions behind an area.
- Factoring by grouping identifies common design components.
- Quadratic factoring can reveal the dimensions of an unknown rectangular panel.

## 4. Learning objectives

The lab must directly teach all four Algebra 1 topics.

### A. Adding and Subtracting Polynomials
- Classify polynomials by degree.
- Classify polynomials by number of terms.
- Identify monomials, binomials and trinomials.
- Add polynomials.
- Subtract polynomials.
- Distribute a negative sign when subtracting.

### B. Multiplying Polynomials
- Multiply monomials.
- Multiply a monomial by a polynomial.
- Multiply binomials.
- Multiply polynomials with multiple terms.
- Apply the square-of-a-sum pattern.
- Apply the difference-of-squares pattern.

### C. Factoring Expressions
- Factor out the greatest common factor.
- Factor by grouping.
- Check a factorization by multiplying back.

### D. Factoring Quadratics
- Factor trinomials in the form x² + bx + c.
- Factor trinomials in the form ax² + bx + c.
- Factor perfect-square trinomials.
- Factor differences of squares.
- Verify factors by expanding.

# PART III — THE FOUR LEARNING STAGES

# STAGE 01: DESIGN THE PROTECTION KIT
## Adding and Subtracting Polynomials

## 9. Mission

The student is the lead pattern designer.

They need to plan the material required for the top tube, down tube and rear triangle protection panels.

Different panels have different modeled areas, and the student must combine or subtract these areas to calculate the total material requirements.

This is the introduction to polynomial classification, addition and subtraction.

### Important modeling convention

Use simplified rectangular design templates and abstract area units.

For mathematical examples, let x represent a variable design dimension measured in design units. Coefficients and constants represent the corresponding design units required to keep the area expressions consistent.

These models are educational layouts, not exact measurements of real curved carbon or aluminum bike frames.

## Activity 1 — Classify the Design Patterns

Introduce different polynomial expressions on protective panel cards.

The student needs to classify each expression by:
- Degree.
- Number of terms.

Examples:

| Expression | Degree | Number of terms |
|---|---|---|
| `7x³` | Cubic | Monomial |
| `3x² - 4x` | Quadratic | Binomial |
| `x² + 5x + 6` | Quadratic | Trinomial |
| `-4x` | Linear | Monomial |
| `9` | Constant | Monomial |
| `2x³ + 3x² + x + 8` | Cubic | Four terms |

Teach that:
- The degree is the greatest exponent of the variable.
- A monomial has one term.
- A binomial has two terms.
- A trinomial has three terms.

Make the terms visually distinct.

Use a drag-and-drop sorting experience where students place expressions into the correct categories.

Include a few expressions that are not polynomials, such as `3/x`, so students learn that polynomial expressions cannot have variables in denominators.

Keep the main objective focused on classification rather than advanced polynomial theory.

## Activity 2 — Combine the Protection Panels

Introduce two different protective film panel designs.

Top tube panel:

`A(x) = 3x² + 4x + 2`

Down tube panel:

`B(x) = 2x² + 5x + 1`

The student must calculate the total modeled design area.

Set up:

`A(x) + B(x)`

`= (3x² + 4x + 2) + (2x² + 5x + 1)`

Combine like terms:

`= 5x² + 9x + 3`

Use a visual design board that groups:
- x² area tiles.
- x area tiles.
- Constant area tiles.

The student should physically or digitally group matching terms before combining their coefficients.

Explain why only like terms can be combined.

## Activity 3 — Calculate Remaining Material

The studio has an available stock of protective film represented by:

`S(x) = 8x² + 7x + 5`

A completed batch of panels uses:

`U(x) = 3x² + 2x + 1`

Ask:

"How much modeled material remains?"

Set up:

`S(x) - U(x)`

`= (8x² + 7x + 5) - (3x² + 2x + 1)`

Subtract:

`= 5x² + 5x + 4`

Show the stock material decreasing as panels are removed from the inventory.

## Activity 4 — Distribute the Negative

This is an especially important part of polynomial subtraction.

Give the student:

`(6x² + 5x + 2) - (2x² - 3x + 4)`

Ask them to identify why the negative sign must be distributed across every term in the second polynomial.

Correct transformation:

`6x² + 5x + 2 - 2x² + 3x - 4`

Simplify:

`4x² + 8x - 2`

Create an interactive challenge where students click each term in the second polynomial and apply the negative sign.

Explain the common mistake of changing the first term's sign but forgetting the remaining terms.

## Activity 5 — Design Your Material Kit

The student receives a collection of protective panel cards.

Each panel has a polynomial area expression.

They must:
1. Classify the expression.
2. Combine two panels.
3. Subtract a removed section.
4. Calculate the remaining material.
5. Explain what the simplified expression means.

Include new numbers in the question bank to prevent memorization.

### Stage completion

Award:

`PATTERN DESIGNER` badge.

# STAGE 02: EXPAND THE GRAPHICS
## Multiplying Polynomials

## 10. Mission

The student has completed the pattern layout.

Now they need to expand different graphic panels into their individual sections so the protection film can be prepared for production.

Introduce an interactive area-model design board.

This should be one of the most visually engaging stages in the lab.

## Activity 1 — The Area Model

Introduce a rectangular protective panel with dimensions:

`(x + 3)` and `(x + 4)`

Ask:

"What is the total area of this design panel?"

Display the rectangle divided into four regions.

The areas are:

- `x × x = x²`
- `x × 4 = 4x`
- `3 × x = 3x`
- `3 × 4 = 12`

Combine:

`x² + 4x + 3x + 12`

Answer:

`x² + 7x + 12`

Show how multiplying the two binomials produces a trinomial.

The student should be able to adjust the side dimensions and see the area expression update.

## Activity 2 — Multiply a Monomial by a Polynomial

Introduce:

`3x(2x + 5)`

Explain that the monomial multiplies every term in the polynomial.

`3x × 2x = 6x²`

`3x × 5 = 15x`

Answer:

`6x² + 15x`

Use an expanded protective-film strip with three repeated design sections.

Provide additional challenges:
- `2x(x + 3)`
- `4x(2x + 1)`
- `3a(a + 4)`

## Activity 3 — Multiply Two Binomials

Introduce the standard expansion:

`(x + 3)(x + 4)`

Students should:
- Multiply first terms.
- Multiply outer terms.
- Multiply inner terms.
- Multiply last terms.
- Combine like terms.

Use the area model as the primary approach, with FOIL as an optional memory aid.

Correct result:

`x² + 7x + 12`

Require students to understand why all four products are necessary.

## Activity 4 — Special Product: Square of a Sum

Create a square graphic panel.

Each side measures:

`x + 4`

The student must expand:

`(x + 4)²`

Show the square divided into four sections.

Areas:
- `x²`
- `4x`
- `4x`
- `16`

Answer:

`x² + 8x + 16`

Introduce the identity:

`(a + b)² = a² + 2ab + b²`

Explain the double middle term visually.

Do not teach this as simply squaring the first term and the second term separately.

## Activity 5 — Special Product: Difference of Squares

Introduce a square design sheet with a smaller square removed.

The outer square has side length x.

The inner square has side length 5.

The remaining area is:

`x² - 25`

Explain that this difference of two square areas can be rearranged into a rectangular area model.

Factorized dimensions:

`(x + 5)(x - 5)`

Therefore:

`(x + 5)(x - 5) = x² - 25`

Use the visual rearrangement to demonstrate the identity.

State that the physical area example assumes x is greater than 5 so that the inner square fits within the outer square.

Include the general pattern:

`(a + b)(a - b) = a² - b²`

## Activity 6 — Multiply Polynomials with More Terms

Introduce a more advanced symbolic production model.

Use:

`(x + 2)(x² + 3x + 4)`

Distribute:

`x(x² + 3x + 4) + 2(x² + 3x + 4)`

Expand:

`x³ + 3x² + 4x + 2x² + 6x + 8`

Combine:

`x³ + 5x² + 10x + 8`

Explain how polynomial multiplication works even when one of the expressions has three terms.

Use a polynomial multiplication grid with the individual products displayed in cells.

This activity is a symbolic algebra challenge rather than a literal physical area measurement.

## Activity 7 — The Graphic Expansion Challenge

Provide a mixed question bank.

| Expression | Answer |
|---|---|
| `2x(x + 3)` | `2x² + 6x` |
| `(x + 2)(x + 5)` | `x² + 7x + 10` |
| `(x + 4)²` | `x² + 8x + 16` |
| `(x + 5)(x - 5)` | `x² - 25` |
| `(2x + 1)(x + 3)` | `2x² + 7x + 3` |
| `(x + 2)(x² + 3x + 4)` | `x³ + 5x² + 10x + 8` |

Include:
- Area-model interactions.
- Missing product challenges.
- Polynomial grids.
- Numeric response.
- Expression construction.
- Error correction.

### Stage completion

Award:

`GRAPHICS EXPANSION SPECIALIST` badge.

# STAGE 03: OPTIMIZE THE CUT SHEETS
## Factoring Expressions

## 11. Mission

The design is complete, but the production team needs to organize the protective film patterns efficiently.

Some designs contain repeated dimensions and shared factors.

The student must identify the greatest common factor and simplify complex expressions by grouping.

This is where they learn that factoring reverses polynomial multiplication.

## Activity 1 — Find the Greatest Common Factor

Present a collection of protective film panels.

Example:

`12x + 18`

Ask:

"What is the greatest common factor shared by both terms?"

Correct answer:

`6`

Factor:

`12x + 18 = 6(2x + 3)`

Explain that both terms have a common factor of six.

Use a material layout where the original expression is represented by 12 x-area sections and 18 unit sections.

Group them into six identical groups.

Show the resulting factored expression.

## Activity 2 — Factor More Complex Expressions

Introduce:

`6x² + 9x`

Find the greatest common factor:

`3x`

Factor:

`3x(2x + 3)`

Give the student additional challenges:

- `8x + 12`
- `10x² + 15x`
- `12x³ + 8x²`
- `6a² + 12a`

For each problem:
1. Find the numerical GCF.
2. Find the variable GCF.
3. Factor out the full common factor.
4. Check by multiplying back.

## Activity 3 — Factoring by Grouping

Introduce four groups of graphic layout components.

Expression:

`3x + 6 + 2xy + 4y`

Group:

`(3x + 6) + (2xy + 4y)`

Factor each group:

`3(x + 2) + 2y(x + 2)`

Identify the common binomial:

`(x + 2)`

Final factorization:

`(x + 2)(3 + 2y)`

Explain that the student is identifying a shared pattern across two groups.

Use a two-column visual design board to show the grouping.

## Activity 4 — The Grouping Workshop

Give students additional examples:

`x² + 5x + 3x + 15`

Group:

`(x² + 5x) + (3x + 15)`

Factor:

`x(x + 5) + 3(x + 5)`

Final:

`(x + 3)(x + 5)`

Additional challenges:

- `2x² + 6x + 5x + 15`
- `3x² + 9x + 2x + 6`
- `4x² + 8x + 3x + 6`

Require students to identify common binomial factors.

## Activity 5 — Check the Pattern

Introduce:

`6x(2x + 3)`

Ask students to multiply back.

Correct:

`12x² + 18x`

Explain that factoring and expanding are reverse processes.

Every factoring question should support a verification action.

### Stage completion

Award:

`CUT SHEET OPTIMIZER` badge.

# STAGE 04: REVERSE ENGINEER THE PANEL
## Factoring Quadratics

## 12. Mission

A new customer has brought the studio a partially completed frame protection design.

They know the total area of several panels, but the original dimensions are missing.

The student must work backward from a polynomial area expression to find the panel dimensions.

This is the central learning experience for factoring quadratics.

Create a large interactive rectangular area model.

## Activity 1 — Recover the Original Panel Dimensions

Present:

`x² + 7x + 12`

The student knows this represents the area of a rectangular panel.

Ask:

"What two binomials could multiply together to produce this trinomial?"

Introduce factor pairs of 12.

Find two numbers that:
- Multiply to 12.
- Add to 7.

Numbers:

3 and 4.

Therefore:

`x² + 7x + 12 = (x + 3)(x + 4)`

Show the dimensions appearing on the rectangle.

The student must understand that factoring reveals the original dimensions.

## Activity 2 — The Factor Pair Finder

Create an interactive challenge.

Given:

`x² + bx + c`

Students must identify:
- Two numbers whose product is c.
- Two numbers whose sum is b.

Use:
- Positive coefficients.
- Negative coefficients.
- Mixed positive and negative factor pairs.

Examples:

`x² + 5x + 6`

Answer:

`(x + 2)(x + 3)`

`x² - x - 12`

Answer:

`(x - 4)(x + 3)`

`x² - 7x + 12`

Answer:

`(x - 3)(x - 4)`

Create a factor-pair selection interface before allowing students to build the final factors.

## Activity 3 — Factor Trinomials with Leading Coefficients

Introduce:

`2x² + 7x + 3`

Explain that these expressions require a slightly different strategy.

Find two numbers that:
- Multiply to `a × c = 2 × 3 = 6`.
- Add to the middle coefficient, 7.

Numbers:

6 and 1.

Split the middle term:

`2x² + 6x + x + 3`

Group:

`(2x² + 6x) + (x + 3)`

Factor:

`2x(x + 3) + 1(x + 3)`

Final:

`(2x + 1)(x + 3)`

Build a visual area model showing:
- Two x² blocks.
- Seven x blocks.
- Three unit blocks.

Animate the blocks being organized into a rectangle with dimensions `(2x + 1)` and `(x + 3)`.

Include additional examples with factorable integer coefficients.

## Activity 4 — Perfect Square Trinomials

Introduce a square panel.

Its side length is:

`x + 5`

The area:

`(x + 5)²`

Expand:

`x² + 10x + 25`

Now reverse the process.

Given:

`x² + 10x + 25`

Factor:

`(x + 5)²`

Introduce the perfect square pattern:

`a² + 2ab + b² = (a + b)²`

Also cover:

`a² - 2ab + b² = (a - b)²`

Example:

`x² - 6x + 9 = (x - 3)²`

Require students to identify perfect-square trinomials from their coefficients.

## Activity 5 — Difference of Squares

Introduce the square panel with a smaller square removed.

The outer area is:

`x²`

The removed inner area is:

`25`

Remaining area:

`x² - 25`

Factor:

`(x + 5)(x - 5)`

Explain the special pattern:

`a² - b² = (a + b)(a - b)`

Include examples:

- `x² - 9`
- `x² - 49`
- `4x² - 25`
- `9x² - 16`

Show the difference-of-squares area rearrangement.

Avoid attempting to factor sums of squares using this pattern.

## Activity 6 — Reverse Engineering Challenge

Give the student a series of completed area models with missing side dimensions.

They must:
1. Read the area polynomial.
2. Identify the correct factor pair.
3. Write the factorization.
4. Label the rectangle dimensions.
5. Multiply back to verify.

Mix:
- Monic trinomials.
- Non-monic trinomials.
- Perfect squares.
- Differences of squares.

Include some expressions that do not factor over the integers, and teach students to recognize when an integer factorization is unavailable rather than forcing an incorrect answer.

### Stage completion

Award:

`MASTER PATTERN ENGINEER` badge.

## 18. Progressive hints

Example:

Question:

Factor:

`x² + 7x + 12`

Hint 1:

"What two numbers multiply to make 12?"

Hint 2:

"Those same two numbers need to add up to 7."

Hint 3:

"Try 3 and 4."

Reveal:

`(x + 3)(x + 4)`

Hints should teach the reasoning process.

For subtraction, explicitly teach how distributing a negative changes every term inside the parentheses.

For factoring, require students to check their result by multiplying back.

## 19. Feedback

Correct:

"Exactly! The two dimensions multiply to give the original panel area. You've successfully reversed the polynomial multiplication."

Incorrect:

"Not quite. Remember that the middle coefficient comes from adding the cross-products. Let's look at the four areas inside the rectangle."

For GCF:

"Look for the largest factor shared by every term, including the variable part."

"Check whether the first and final terms are perfect squares. Then see whether the middle term is twice their product."

- Non-monic quadratic factoring.

### Polynomial representation

Use a structured internal representation such as a coefficient map indexed by exponent.

For example:

`3x² + 4x + 2`

Represent as:
- Power 2: coefficient 3.
- Power 1: coefficient 4.
- Power 0: coefficient 2.

This enables deterministic addition, subtraction, multiplication and simplification.

### Factoring

Support:
- Integer coefficient factors.
- Greatest common factor extraction.
- Grouping.
- Integer factor-pair searches for quadratics.
- Special identities.

For trinomials with integer factors, verify the factorization by expanding it back into the original polynomial.

For expressions that do not factor over the integers, clearly indicate that no integer factorization is available.

Do not present an incorrect guessed factorization.

### Area models

The area visualization must be connected to the actual polynomial engine.

The sum of the visual regions must match the expanded expression.

The factored dimensions must multiply back to the original polynomial.

Do not use pixel dimensions as mathematical measurements.

### Units and assumptions

Use abstract design units for polynomial tiles and clearly labelled instructional area units for the frame-protection examples.

Do not imply that the 2D rectangles represent the true curved surface of an individual bicycle frame.

The student should finish Wrap Lab with a custom MTB frame protection concept they are genuinely excited about.
