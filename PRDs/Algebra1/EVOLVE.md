# PART I — PRODUCT VISION

## 1. The concept

The student becomes a junior product development engineer at a fictional mountain bike company called Northline Cycles.

The company has an ambitious project:

Develop and launch a next-generation gravity mountain bike called **Project Switchback**.

The student joins the product development team and works through four departments:

1. Prototype Design Lab.
2. Engineering Test Lab.
3. Product Launch.
4. Used Market Analysis.

Their job is to help the company take a new mountain bike from its initial prototype to its product launch and eventual resale market.

Along the way, they encounter mathematical problems involving:
- Exponent rules.
- Scientific notation.
- Exponential growth.
- Exponential decay.

The student experiences the roles of a product design engineer, test technician, product development manager and market analyst.

## 2. Educational philosophy and Algebra 1 scope

This is an Algebra 1 learning experience.

All mathematical explanations, examples and challenges must remain appropriate for introductory high-school algebra.

### Required mathematical scope

**Exponent Rules**
- Understand exponents as repeated multiplication.
- Apply the product rule.
- Apply the quotient rule.
- Apply the power-of-a-power rule.
- Apply the power-of-a-product rule.
- Simplify zero exponents.
- Simplify negative integer exponents.

**Scientific Notation**
- Convert between standard notation and scientific notation.
- Understand positive and negative powers of ten.
- Multiply numbers in scientific notation.
- Divide numbers in scientific notation.
- Compare large and small quantities.

**Exponential Growth**
- Recognize exponential growth in tables, graphs and equations.
- Understand initial values and growth factors.
- Write a growth model from a starting quantity and percentage rate.
- Evaluate exponential models.
- Distinguish linear growth from exponential growth.

**Exponential Decay**
- Recognize exponential decay.
- Understand decay factors and percentage decrease.
- Write a decay model.
- Evaluate an exponential decay model.
- Use tables and graphs to estimate when a quantity reaches a target.
- Distinguish linear decay from exponential decay.

### Important curriculum boundaries

Do not introduce:
- Logarithmic functions.
- Logarithmic equations.
- Solving exponential equations using logarithms.
- Fractional or rational exponents.
- Advanced exponential transformations.
- Complex engineering calculations.
- Advanced mechanical engineering.

Students should learn to estimate target values using tables, graphs and repeated substitution rather than logarithmic algebra.

Keep the emphasis on understanding patterns, applying rules and interpreting exponential models.

## 3. The MTB industry connection

The experience is inspired by real mountain bike product development.

Companies such as SRAM and RockShox use engineering, prototyping, laboratory testing and product development to bring new components to market.

RockShox has documented how its engineers use testing machines to replay actual ride-data files and simulate repeated bumps, drops and movements. Some structural and durability tests can run for 28–52 hours.

This provides authentic inspiration for the Engineering Test Lab.

Use real MTB engineering environments as visual inspiration, but create a fictional product, company, test data and engineering measurements.

All educational numbers must be clearly identified as illustrative classroom values rather than actual manufacturer specifications.

## 4. Central storyline

"Northline Cycles is developing its next generation of gravity mountain bikes.

You've joined the product development team, and you're responsible for helping create Project Switchback.

You'll use mathematics to understand component scaling, investigate laboratory test results, prepare for the product launch and track the bike's value in the used market.

From tiny measurements to growing product interest, the numbers tell the story of how a new mountain bike comes to life.

Welcome to Project Evolve."

# PART III — STAGE IMPLEMENTATION

# STAGE 01: PROTOTYPE DESIGN LAB
## Exponent Rules

## 8. Mission

The student joins the product design team.

Their first job is to investigate mathematical expressions used in component scaling and CAD-inspired prototype models.

Introduce an interactive virtual design environment containing:
- A suspension fork prototype.
- A rectangular calibration block.
- A cubic measurement model.
- Component dimension labels.
- Engineering expression panels.

The goal is to help students understand exponents as repeated multiplication and learn how exponent rules simplify calculations.

The visual environment can resemble CAD software, but the student does not need to operate real engineering software.

### Activity 1 — Understand Exponents

Introduce a simple calibration block.

Each edge has a length of 2 units.

Ask:

"How many unit cubes make up this larger cube?"

Show:

`2 × 2 × 2`

Introduce exponential notation:

`2³ = 8`

Explain:
- 2 is the base.
- 3 is the exponent.
- The exponent tells us how many times the base is multiplied by itself.

Animate the cube as its layers appear.

Then introduce a variable-based example:

`x³ = x × x × x`

Keep this as a visual introduction to repeated multiplication.

### Activity 2 — Product Rule

Show a CAD model with two groups of identical scale factors.

`x³ × x²`

Explain that multiplying powers with the same base means combining all the factors.

`x³ × x² = x⁵`

Introduce:

`a^m × a^n = a^(m+n)`

Use a visual where students combine repeated factors.

Give a progressively challenging question bank:

- `x² × x³`
- `a⁴ × a²`
- `y³ × y⁵`
- `m² × m⁴`

Require students to identify the base and add the exponents.

Do not teach product rules for different bases as though the exponents can be combined.

### Activity 3 — Quotient Rule

Show:

`x⁵ / x²`

Animate the common factors canceling.

Remaining expression:

`x³`

Introduce:

`a^m / a^n = a^(m-n)`

For nonzero a.

Examples:

- `x⁶ / x² = x⁴`
- `a⁷ / a³ = a⁴`
- `y⁵ / y² = y³`
- `m⁸ / m⁴ = m⁴`

Students select which factors cancel and simplify the expression.

### Activity 4 — Power of a Power

Show:

`(x²)³`

Explain that the entire x-squared expression is multiplied by itself three times.

`x² × x² × x²`

Result:

`x⁶`

Introduce:

`(a^m)^n = a^(mn)`

Examples:
- `(x²)³ = x⁶`
- `(a³)² = a⁶`
- `(y²)⁴ = y⁸`

Require students to multiply the exponents rather than add them.

### Activity 5 — Power of a Product

Return to the calibration block.

Show:

`(2x)³`

Expand:

`(2x)(2x)(2x)`

Result:

`8x³`

Introduce:

`(ab)^n = a^n b^n`

Examples:
- `(2x)² = 4x²`
- `(3a)² = 9a²`
- `(2y)³ = 8y³`

Visually explain that the exponent applies to every factor inside the parentheses.

### Activity 6 — Zero Exponents

Introduce:

`a⁰ = 1`, where `a ≠ 0`.

Explain that a nonzero base raised to the zero power equals one.

Use a series of simple examples:
- `5⁰ = 1`
- `10⁰ = 1`
- `x⁰ = 1`, for nonzero x.
- `(3a)⁰ = 1`, when the base is nonzero.

Ask students to identify common zero-exponent mistakes.

Do not define zero to the zero power as one.

### Activity 7 — Negative Exponents

Introduce:

`a^-n = 1/a^n`, where `a ≠ 0`.

The important teaching point:

**A negative exponent means a reciprocal, not a negative value.**

Examples:

`x^-2 = 1/x²`

`x^-3 = 1/x³`

`10^-2 = 0.01`

`10^-4 = 0.0001`

Build a visual precision scale showing how values become smaller as negative powers of ten become more negative.

Students should practice rewriting expressions with negative exponents using positive exponents.

### Activity 8 — The Prototype Design Challenge

Create a mixed-skill assessment.

| Expression | Correct answer |
|---|---|
| `x² × x³` | `x⁵` |
| `x⁶ / x²` | `x⁴` |
| `(x²)³` | `x⁶` |
| `(2x)³` | `8x³` |
| `(3a)²` | `9a²` |
| `7⁰` | 1 |
| `x^-2` | `1/x²` |
| `10^-3` | 0.001 |

Include:
- Simplification.
- Rule identification.
- Matching.
- Multiple choice.
- Numerical evaluation.
- Short explanations.

Require students to identify which exponent law they are using.

Award:

# STAGE 02: ENGINEERING TEST LAB
## Scientific Notation

## 9. Mission

The prototype is now assembled.

The student moves into the engineering testing laboratory.

Their task is to analyze huge sensor datasets and extremely small measurement values.

Build a visually engaging test environment with:
- Suspension fork attached to a test machine.
- Sensor monitoring screens.
- Digital measurement instruments.
- Data counters.
- Precision measurement displays.

### Activity 1 — The Massive Test Dataset

Introduce a fictional test scenario.

The machine operates for 28 hours.

For this educational example, assume:
- 200 sensor readings per second.
- 60 seconds per minute.
- 60 minutes per hour.

Ask:

"How many sensor readings are collected during the complete test?"

Expression:

`200 × 28 × 60 × 60`

Correct answer:

`20,160,000`

Convert:

`2.016 × 10⁷`

Explain why scientific notation is useful for representing very large quantities.

Label the 200 readings per second as a fictional educational assumption, not an actual manufacturer's testing specification.

### Activity 2 — Tiny Measurements

Introduce a fictional precision inspection reading:

`0.0000045 meters`

Ask students to convert it into scientific notation.

Correct answer:

`4.5 × 10⁻⁶ meters`

Show the decimal movement.

Then introduce:

`0.000012 meters`

Correct answer:

`1.2 × 10⁻⁵ meters`

Ask students which is larger.

Use visual comparisons of scale.

Clearly identify all measurements as classroom examples rather than actual manufacturing tolerances.

### Activity 3 — Scientific Notation Converter

Build an interactive converter.

Students should be able to:
- Enter a standard number.
- Move the decimal point.
- Change the exponent.
- See the corresponding scientific notation.
- Reverse the conversion.

Required examples:

| Standard notation | Scientific notation |
|---|---|
| 20,160,000 | `2.016 × 10⁷` |
| 350,000 | `3.5 × 10⁵` |
| 0.0000045 | `4.5 × 10⁻⁶` |
| 0.00000072 | `7.2 × 10⁻⁷` |

Teach normalized scientific notation:

`a × 10ⁿ`

For standard normalized notation, the absolute value of a must be at least 1 and less than 10.

### Activity 4 — Multiplication

Introduce:

`(3.0 × 10⁵)(2.0 × 10³)`

Multiply the coefficients:

`3.0 × 2.0 = 6.0`

Add the exponents:

`10⁵ × 10³ = 10⁸`

Answer:

`6.0 × 10⁸`

Students should see the coefficient multiplication and exponent addition separately.

### Activity 5 — Division

Use the fictional test dataset.

Total readings:

`2.016 × 10⁷`

The data is divided into:

`4.0 × 10²`

Equal files.

Calculate:

`(2.016 × 10⁷) / (4.0 × 10²)`

Divide coefficients:

`2.016 / 4.0 = 0.504`

Subtract exponents:

`10^(7-2) = 10⁵`

Normalize:

`5.04 × 10⁴`

Answer:

`50,400 readings per file`

### Activity 6 — Compare Engineering Measurements

Show students two measurements.

They must:
- Compare powers of ten.
- Compare coefficients where necessary.
- Identify larger and smaller measurements.
- Convert values into standard notation when appropriate.

Use a mix of large and small quantities.

### Activity 7 — Engineering Test Challenge

Include a mixed practice set covering:
- Standard notation.
- Scientific notation.
- Decimal movement.
- Positive exponents.
- Negative exponents.
- Multiplication.
- Division.
- Comparing magnitude.

Require students to preserve appropriate units.

### Stage completion

Award:

`TEST ENGINEER` badge.

# STAGE 03: PRODUCT LAUNCH
## Exponential Growth

## 10. Mission

Project Switchback has completed its prototype development.

The company is preparing to launch the new gravity mountain bike.

The student becomes product launch manager.

Their job is to model how interest in the new bike grows during the launch campaign.

Introduce:
- Rider ambassador announcements.
- Product teaser videos.
- Beta tester registrations.
- Early-access waitlists.
- Social media launch campaigns.

All numerical growth data must be clearly identified as fictional educational scenarios.

### Activity 1 — The Launch Waitlist

The company begins with 500 interested riders.

The waitlist grows by 20% each month.

Ask:

"How many people will be on the waitlist after several months?"

Introduce:

`N(t) = 500(1.20)^t`

Explain:
- N = estimated waitlist size.
- t = number of months.
- 500 = initial number of interested riders.
- 1.20 = growth factor.
- 20% = monthly growth rate.

Teach:

A 20% increase means the new quantity is 120% of the previous quantity.

Convert:

`120% = 1.20`

The quantity is multiplied by 1.20 each month.

### Activity 2 — Build the Growth Table

Create an interactive launch tracker.

| Month | Estimated waitlist |
|---:|---:|
| 0 | 500 |
| 1 | 600 |
| 2 | 720 |
| 3 | 864 |
| 4 | 1,036.8 |

For a real-world person count, display approximately 1,037 riders at month four while preserving the model's numerical value internally.

Require students to evaluate the model.

Example:

`N(3) = 500(1.20)³`

Answer:

`N(3) = 864`

### Activity 3 — Linear or Exponential?

Compare two fictional launch campaigns.

**Campaign A — Linear**

Adds 100 interested riders every month.

`L(t) = 500 + 100t`

**Campaign B — Exponential**

Increases by 20% every month.

`N(t) = 500(1.20)^t`

At month four:

Linear:

`L(4) = 900`

Exponential:

`N(4) = 1036.8`

Display both graphs.

Explain:

- Linear growth adds the same amount each period.
- Exponential growth multiplies by the same factor each period.
- The exponential model's rate of increase becomes larger as the quantity grows.

The student must identify both relationships using:
- Tables.
- Equations.
- Graphs.
- Written descriptions.

### Activity 4 — Identify the Growth Factor

Present:

`N(t) = 100(1.15)^t`

Growth factor: 1.15.

Growth rate: 15%.

And:

`N(t) = 250(1.30)^t`

Growth factor: 1.30.

Growth rate: 30%.

And:

`N(t) = 1000(1.05)^t`

Growth factor: 1.05.

Growth rate: 5%.

Require students to identify:
- Initial value.
- Growth factor.
- Growth percentage.

### Activity 5 — Write Your Own Growth Model

Present the following description:

"A new gravity bike begins with 800 interested riders on its early-access list. The list grows by 10% each month."

Ask the student to write the exponential model.

Correct:

`N(t) = 800(1.10)^t`

Then ask them to calculate the estimated waitlist size after three months.

Make students identify the initial value and growth factor before entering the equation.

### Activity 6 — Launch Momentum Simulator

Build an interactive dashboard.

Allow the student to change:
- Starting waitlist.
- Growth percentage.
- Number of months.

Update:
- Formula.
- Growth factor.
- Table of values.
- Graph.
- Projected waitlist.

Include a linear comparison.

Do not imply that actual product launches always grow exponentially. Explain that this is a simplified mathematical model for learning how repeated percentage growth works.

Award:

`PRODUCT LAUNCH MANAGER` badge.

# STAGE 04: THE USED MARKET
## Exponential Decay

## 11. Mission

Project Switchback has been released.

The student now becomes a used-market analyst.

They must understand how a bike's modeled value changes as it gets older.

This creates a practical way to understand exponential decay through repeated percentage reductions.

### Activity 1 — The Starting Value

Introduce the fictional Project Switchback build.

Starting value:

`$6,000`

For this classroom model, assume the bike retains 80% of its modeled value each year.

Therefore, it loses 20% of its value each year.

Introduce:

`V(t) = 6000(0.80)^t`

Explain:
- V = modeled value.
- t = age in years.
- 6000 = initial value.
- 0.80 = annual retention factor.
- 20% = annual decrease.

Explain that the model multiplies by 0.80 each year rather than subtracting a fixed $1,200 every year.

### Activity 2 — Build the Depreciation Table

| Year | Modeled value |
|---:|---:|
| 0 | $6,000 |
| 1 | $4,800 |
| 2 | $3,840 |
| 3 | $3,072 |
| 4 | $2,457.60 |
| 5 | $1,966.08 |

Require students to calculate the values through substitution.

Display a bike value indicator that decreases as time progresses.

### Activity 3 — Linear vs Exponential Decay

Compare:

Linear:

`L(t) = 6000 - 1200t`

Exponential:

`V(t) = 6000(0.80)^t`

Explain:
- Linear decay subtracts the same amount each year.
- Exponential decay decreases by the same percentage each year.
- The resulting graphs have different shapes.

Let the student switch between the two models and compare their tables and graphs.

### Activity 4 — Estimate When the Bike Reaches a Target Value

The company wants to estimate when the bike's modeled value drops below $2,500.

Use:

`V(t) = 6000(0.80)^t`

Show the relevant table values:

Year 3:

`V(3) = $3,072`

Year 4:

`V(4) = $2,457.60`

Ask:

"Between which two years does the bike's modeled value fall below $2,500?"

Answer: Between years three and four.

If the question asks for the first whole-year checkpoint below $2,500, the answer is year four.

Use a graph to visually identify the target crossing.

**Important Algebra 1 restriction:** Do not solve this by introducing logarithms. Students should estimate using a table, repeated substitution or graph.

### Activity 5 — Change the Depreciation Rate

Create an interactive depreciation simulator.

Allow the student to modify:
- Initial value.
- Annual percentage decrease.
- Number of years.

Compare:
- 10% annual decrease.
- 15% annual decrease.
- 20% annual decrease.
- 25% annual decrease.

Display how the curves change.

Ask:
- Which bike retains more of its modeled value?
- Which model falls below $3,000 first?
- What happens when the depreciation rate increases?

### Activity 6 — Real-World Model Limitations

Explain that actual used-bike prices depend on:
- Condition.
- Brand and model.
- Component specification.
- Maintenance.
- Model-year changes.
- Market demand.
- Seasonality.

Explain that this model intentionally simplifies the situation to teach repeated percentage decay.

The fixed 20% rate is not an actual universal depreciation rate or a used-bike valuation forecast.

Award:

`MARKET ANALYST` badge.

## 26. Mathematical engine

All calculations must be deterministic and testable.

Do not use JavaScript `eval()` for student-generated expressions.

Support:
- Integer exponents.
- Positive exponents.
- Zero exponents.
- Negative integer exponents.
- Product and quotient rules.
- Power of a power.
- Power of a product.
- Scientific notation conversions.
- Scientific notation multiplication and division.
- Exponential growth models.
- Exponential decay models.
- Tables of values.
- Graphing.
- Target-value estimation.

### Exponent handling

Ensure:
- Exponents apply to the correct base or grouped expression.
- Product and quotient rules are used only with compatible bases.
- Negative exponents create reciprocals.
- Zero-exponent rules are applied only to nonzero bases.
- Division by zero is prevented.
- Expressions with zero to the zero power are not treated as ordinary exponent-rule examples.

### Scientific notation

Use normalized scientific notation:

`a × 10^n`

Where:

`1 ≤ |a| < 10`

For positive quantities, the coefficient is between 1 and 10, not including 10.

Support positive and negative integer exponents.

### Exponential models

Growth:

`y = a(1 + r)^t`

Decay:

`y = a(1 - r)^t`

Where:
- a = initial quantity.
- r = percentage rate in decimal form.
- t = number of elapsed periods.

For growth:
- r is a positive growth rate.

For decay:
- r is a percentage decrease between 0 and 1.

Clearly distinguish the percentage rate from the growth or retention factor.

Do not use logarithms in this unit.

Target-value estimates must use tables, graphs or repeated substitution.

### Numerical precision

Preserve full internal precision.

Round only for display or where the question explicitly requires an estimate.

For people, inventory and other indivisible quantities, explain when a displayed model value must be rounded to a whole number.

| Calculation | Expected result |
|---|---|
| 20,160,000 | `2.016 × 10⁷` |
| 0.0000045 | `4.5 × 10⁻⁶` |
| 0.00000072 | `7.2 × 10⁻⁷` |

### Exponential Growth

Model:

`N(t) = 500(1.20)^t`

| Month | Expected value |
|---:|---:|
| 0 | 500 |
| 1 | 600 |
| 2 | 720 |
| 3 | 864 |
| 4 | 1036.8 |

Linear comparison:

`L(t) = 500 + 100t`

At month 4:

`L(4) = 900`

Exponential:

`N(4) = 1036.8`

Ensure the application distinguishes equal-amount growth from equal-percentage growth.

### Exponential Decay

Model:

`V(t) = 6000(0.80)^t`

| Year | Expected value |
|---:|---:|
| 0 | $6,000 |
| 1 | $4,800 |
| 2 | $3,840 |
| 3 | $3,072 |
| 4 | $2,457.60 |
| 5 | $1,966.08 |

At the $2,500 target:
- Year 3 is above.
- Year 4 is below.
- The target is crossed between years 3 and 4.

Verify that the lab uses a table or graph for this estimate, not logarithms.

## 28. Common misconception testing

**Exponent Rules**
- Adding exponents with different bases.
- Multiplying exponents in the product rule.
- Applying powers incorrectly across parentheses.
- Interpreting negative exponents as negative values.
- Treating zero raised to zero as one.
- Forgetting denominator restrictions.

**Scientific Notation**
- Moving the decimal in the wrong direction.
- Using an incorrect exponent sign.
- Leaving the coefficient outside normalized notation.
- Incorrect exponent addition or subtraction.
- Comparing only coefficients.
- Losing units.

**Exponential Growth**
- Adding the percentage as a fixed quantity.
- Confusing initial value with growth factor.
- Using 0.20 instead of 1.20 as the multiplier for 20% growth.
- Confusing linear and exponential growth.

**Exponential Decay**
- Subtracting a constant dollar amount instead of applying a repeated percentage decrease.
- Confusing depreciation rate with retention factor.
- Using an incorrect decay multiplier.
- Treating the estimate as a guaranteed market valuation.
- Confusing an approximate target crossing with an exact answer.
