# Mathlete MTB — Physics of the Ride
## Complete Physics Curriculum & Learning Roadmap
### Product Requirements Document (PRD)

**Product:** Mathlete MTB  
**Feature:** Physics of the Ride  
**Priority:** Core academic curriculum  
**Curriculum Reference:** Carnegie Mellon University Open Learning Initiative (OLI), College Physics 1 and College Physics 2  
**Primary Audience:** Middle and high school students, especially mountain bikers  
**Implementation:** Claude Code / Full-Stack Development

---

# 1. Product Vision

Build a complete, interactive physics curriculum that teaches students the foundational concepts of physics through mountain biking.

Physics of the Ride should transform the mountain biking experience into a learning environment.

Instead of introducing students to physics through abstract textbook explanations, start with the physical phenomena they encounter in mountain biking:

- Why does a rider accelerate down a trail?
- How does a rider generate enough speed to clear a jump?
- Why does a bike slow down when braking?
- How does suspension absorb impacts?
- Why does a wider cornering line change the forces acting on a bike?
- How does a rider generate power while climbing?
- Why does a tire lose grip?
- How do wheels, gears, and cranks transfer energy?
- How does a hydraulic brake system work?
- How do electric mountain bikes generate power?

Each question provides an opportunity to introduce a fundamental physics concept.

**The mission:** Make students genuinely interested in understanding the science behind the sport they love.

## 1.1 Core Learning Philosophy

Mathlete MTB should teach students to become better problem solvers by using mountain biking as the context for understanding mathematics and science.

The instructional sequence should be:

1. Start with an interesting mountain biking phenomenon.
2. Ask the student to make a prediction.
3. Introduce the physics concept that explains the phenomenon.
4. Explore the concept through an interactive visualization.
5. Apply mathematics to understand the outcome.
6. Complete a challenge using what they learned.
7. Explain their reasoning in their own words.

Students should discover physics rather than simply memorize it.

# 2. Curriculum Scope

Use both OLI College Physics courses as the reference curriculum.

The complete reference sequence contains 28 modules.

## Course 1 — Trail Physics: Mechanics & Energy

**17 modules**

This is the primary course and should be built first.

It covers the mechanics of mountain biking: movement, acceleration, forces, energy, momentum, rotation, fluid systems, suspension, vibrations, waves, and thermodynamics.

## Course 2 — Trail Technology: Electricity, Magnetism & Optics

**11 modules**

This is the advanced course.

It connects physics to the technology used in modern mountain biking, including electric mountain bikes, motors, batteries, sensors, bicycle computers, lighting, optics, and wireless technology.

## Curriculum Organization

Organize the courses into learning units rather than exposing students to 28 unrelated topics.

## UNIT 1 — Physics Foundations

### Module 1: Introduction to Physics

**Reference curriculum:** Course Introduction

**Physics concepts:**
- What physics studies.
- Scientific models.
- Physical laws and theories.
- Physical systems.
- Observations and evidence.
- The relationship between physics and everyday life.

**MTB application:**

Introduce physics through the mountain bike itself.

Ask students:

- Why does a bike move?
- What makes a bike accelerate?
- What happens when a rider hits a jump?
- How does suspension work?
- Why does a bike turn?

Introduce the idea that a mountain bike is a physical system made up of interacting components.

**Interactive experience: The Physics of a Mountain Bike**

Build an interactive illustration of a mountain bike.

Students select different parts of the bike to explore the physics involved:

- Wheels: rotational motion.
- Tires: friction and traction.
- Suspension: springs and oscillations.
- Brakes: friction and energy transfer.
- Drivetrain: torque and mechanical advantage.
- Frame: forces and structural deformation.

**Learning objective:** Students understand that physics can explain the behavior of systems they encounter in everyday life.

### Module 2: Mathematics Review

**Reference curriculum:** Mathematics Review

**Math concepts:**
- Algebra.
- Rearranging equations.
- Solving for unknown variables.
- Ratios and proportions.
- Geometry.
- Trigonometry.
- Sine, cosine, and tangent.

**MTB application:**

Introduce mathematical skills through riding scenarios.

Examples:

- Calculate trail grade using elevation change and horizontal distance.
- Calculate speed given distance and time.
- Calculate a jump's horizontal range.
- Find the height of a jump using a right triangle.
- Rearrange an equation to solve for the speed necessary to achieve a target distance.

**Interactive experience: MTB Math Toolkit**

Build a reusable math practice area that includes:

- Equation rearrangement.
- Unit conversions.
- Fraction and decimal practice.
- Ratios.
- Right-triangle geometry.
- Basic trigonometry.
- Graph interpretation.

The toolkit should be available across all subsequent physics modules.

**Learning objective:** Students develop the mathematical foundation needed to solve physics problems.

### Module 3: Introduction to Physics and Scientific Measurement

**Physics concepts:**
- SI units.
- Derived units.
- Scientific notation.
- Significant figures.
- Measurement uncertainty.
- Estimation.
- Scientific investigation.
- Models and assumptions.

**MTB application:**

Students investigate how physical measurements are collected on a trail.

Examples:

- Measure a jump's height.
- Measure the distance traveled.
- Record speed using a timing gate.
- Estimate slope grade.
- Measure suspension travel.
- Compare calculated speed with GPS speed.

Teach students that measurements have uncertainty and that different measurement tools have different levels of precision.

**Interactive experience: Trail Measurement Lab**

Students select a measurement task and use sample data to calculate quantities.

Include:
- Units and conversions.
- Significant figures.
- Measurement error.
- Percent uncertainty.
- Estimation and rounding.

**Learning objective:** Students understand how to collect, interpret, and evaluate physical data.

# 6. Standardized Lesson Structure

Every module must follow a reusable lesson template.

The goal is to give students a consistent learning experience across the entire curriculum.

## 6.1 Lesson Introduction — The Hook

Start with an engaging mountain biking question.

Example:

"Why does a rider travel farther when they launch from a bigger jump?"

Use a photograph, animation, video, or interactive illustration to establish the phenomenon.

Do not begin with equations.

## 6.2 Discover — Make a Prediction

Ask the student to predict what will happen.

Examples:
- What happens to acceleration when speed doubles?
- What happens to suspension compression if mass increases?
- What happens to jump distance if takeoff speed changes?

Collect their prediction.

Do not immediately reveal the correct answer.

## 6.3 Learn — Understand the Physics

Introduce:
- The physical principle.
- Important vocabulary.
- Relevant equations.
- A visual explanation.
- A real mountain biking example.

Keep lessons broken into short sections.

Avoid presenting several equations without demonstrating their relationships.

## 6.4 Experiment — Interactive Learning

Provide a simulator, visualization, or virtual investigation.

Examples:
- Jump Lab.
- Berm Lab.
- Suspension Lab.
- MTB Power Lab.

Students should manipulate variables and observe changes.

## 6.5 Calculate — Apply Mathematics

Create guided problems using mountain biking scenarios.

Each problem should include:
- Known variables.
- Unknown variable.
- Relevant equation.
- Step-by-step solution.
- Units.
- Final interpretation.

Gradually transition students from guided solutions to independent problem-solving.

## 6.6 Challenge — Demonstrate Understanding

Each module needs at least three initial assessment types:

1. Conceptual multiple-choice question.
2. Quantitative mathematics problem.
3. Interactive prediction or simulation challenge.

Advanced modules should also include open-ended reasoning activities.

## 6.7 Explain — Teach the Student to Think

Ask students to explain their results.

Use a Claim-Evidence-Reasoning structure:

- **Claim:** What did you discover?
- **Evidence:** What data supports your conclusion?
- **Reasoning:** Which physics principle explains the result?

This is particularly important for the Mathlete mission of building problem-solving skills.

# 9. Student Differentiation

The curriculum must accommodate students with different mathematics backgrounds and learning preferences.

Do not force every student through the same explanation or calculation difficulty.

Provide three learning levels.

### Level 1 — Explore

Best for students who need conceptual foundations.

- Visual explanations.
- Interactive demonstrations.
- Minimal equations.
- Guided predictions.
- Basic arithmetic.
- Accessible vocabulary.

### Level 2 — Apply

Best for standard high school physics.

- Algebraic equations.
- Unit conversions.
- Graph interpretation.
- Multi-step calculations.
- Guided problem-solving.

### Level 3 — Engineer

Best for advanced students.

- More complex equations.
- Multi-variable models.
- Trigonometry.
- Experimental uncertainty.
- Model assumptions.
- Independent investigations.
- Advanced simulations.

Students should be able to work at different levels within the same module.

This makes it possible for students with strong athletic understanding but weaker mathematical foundations to engage with the same physics topics as advanced students.

## Scientific Accuracy

Every physics activity must document its mathematical assumptions.

For example:
- Jump Lab initially uses an idealized projectile model.
- Berm Lab uses an idealized banked-turn model.
- Suspension Lab begins with an ideal spring-mass model.
- Brake Heat Lab uses simplified energy-transfer assumptions.

Students should understand the difference between an idealized model and real-world mountain biking.

Do not present educational simulations as real-world performance, equipment setup, or safety recommendations.

- Predict projectile trajectories.
- Apply Newton's laws.
- Explain friction and cornering forces.
- Understand energy transformations.
- Calculate mechanical power.
- Explain momentum and impulse.
- Understand rotation and torque.
- Analyze hydraulic systems.
- Explain suspension behavior.
- Understand waves and temperature.
- Explore electrical and magnetic systems.
- Apply optics and electromagnetic concepts.

# 15. Final Product Vision

Physics of the Ride should become the central academic physics pathway inside Mathlete MTB.

Jump Lab and Berm Lab should not feel like independent games that happen to teach physics.

They should be essential parts of a larger educational experience.

A student might begin by learning about velocity in Trail Speed Lab, move into acceleration and projectile motion in Jump Lab, and then explore centripetal acceleration in Berm Lab.

Later, that same student might investigate suspension in Suspension Lab, torque in Wheel & Gear Lab, and energy transfer in Trail Energy Lab.

The student develops a connected understanding of physics through the sport they love.

**The ultimate goal:**

Build a physics curriculum that students actually want to work through.

Not because they have to complete another physics assignment, but because they are curious about how the sport they love works.
