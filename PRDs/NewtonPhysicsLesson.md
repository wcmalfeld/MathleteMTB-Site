# 1. Lesson Vision

Build an engaging, interactive physics lesson that teaches Newton's three laws of motion through mountain biking.

Students should learn how forces influence the motion of a mountain bike and rider by exploring real-world riding scenarios.

Rather than introducing students to three laws as abstract definitions, begin with questions they can relate to:

- Why does a mountain biker keep moving after they stop pedaling?
- Why does a heavier rider need more force to accelerate at the same rate?
- Why does pushing harder on the pedals make a bike accelerate?
- Why does the ground push back against a mountain bike tire?
- Why does a rider feel a force when landing a jump?
- Why does a bike slow down when the rider stops pedaling?
- What forces act on a bike when climbing a steep trail?

The lesson should guide students from intuitive understanding to mathematical reasoning.

### Core learning objective

By the end of this lesson, students should be able to explain and apply Newton's three laws of motion to mountain biking scenarios using mathematical equations, force diagrams, and physical reasoning.

### Key instructional principle

Every physics concept should answer three questions:

1. What does this mean?
2. Where do we experience it while mountain biking?
3. How can we use mathematics to understand or predict it?

# 2. Learning Objectives

By the end of the lesson, students should be able to:

1. Define force and identify its units.
2. Explain the difference between mass and weight.
3. Identify scalar and vector quantities.
4. Explain balanced and unbalanced forces.
5. Describe Newton's First Law and inertia.
6. Explain how mass influences an object's resistance to changes in motion.
7. Apply Newton's Second Law to calculate acceleration, force, or mass.
8. Describe Newton's Third Law using mountain biking examples.
9. Identify action-reaction force pairs.
10. Construct and interpret basic free-body diagrams.
11. Identify gravity, normal force, friction, and air resistance.
12. Calculate net force acting on a mountain bike system.
13. Explain how forces change when riding on an incline.
14. Use mathematical evidence to explain a physical phenomenon.

# 4. Section 1 — What Are Forces?

## 4.1 Student-Facing Introduction

**Opening question:**

"What makes your mountain bike move?"

When you pedal your mountain bike, something causes it to accelerate.

When you pull the brakes, something causes it to slow down.

When you hit a jump, something changes the direction of your motion.

All of these situations involve forces.

A force is an interaction that can change an object's motion or deform an object.

Forces have both magnitude and direction, which makes them vector quantities.

We measure force in Newtons (N).

## 4.2 Mountain Biking Connections

Introduce the following examples with illustrations or short animations.

| Type of force | Mountain biking example |
|---|---|
| Applied force | The rider applies force to the pedals. |
| Gravitational force | Gravity pulls the rider and bike toward Earth. |
| Normal force | The trail pushes upward against the tires. |
| Friction | The tires interact with the ground to accelerate or brake. |
| Air resistance | Air pushes against the rider as they travel downhill. |
| Spring force | The suspension spring pushes back when compressed. |
| Tension | Tension in a brake cable or drivetrain component transmits force. |

Not every force acts in every riding situation. The student should understand that the physical situation determines which forces are present.

## 4.3 Mass vs. Weight

Introduce an important distinction.

**Mass** measures how much matter an object contains and its inertia.

Unit: kilograms (kg).

**Weight** is the gravitational force acting on an object.

Unit: Newtons (N).

Equation:

W = mg

Where:

- W = weight.
- m = mass.
- g = gravitational acceleration.

Use:

g = 9.81 m/s²

### Example

A rider and bike have a combined mass of 80 kg.

What is their weight?

W = 80 × 9.81

W = 784.8 N

Mountain biking connection:

A heavier rider-bike system experiences a greater gravitational force than a lighter system. This is relevant when studying climbing, jumping, braking, and suspension behavior.

## 4.4 Net Force

Introduce the concept that multiple forces can act on an object simultaneously.

The net force is the vector sum of all external forces acting on the selected system.

For forces along one straight line:

F_net = F_forward − F_backward

Example:

A rider experiences:

- 200 N forward.
- 50 N backward.

Net force:

F_net = 200 − 50

F_net = 150 N forward.

The net force is what determines the object's acceleration.

## 4.5 Balanced and Unbalanced Forces

**Balanced forces:**

The net force is zero.

The object has zero acceleration and therefore maintains constant velocity.

**Unbalanced forces:**

The net force is not zero.

The object accelerates in the direction of the net force.

Mountain biking examples:

- A rider traveling at constant speed on flat ground has approximately balanced horizontal forces.
- A rider accelerating out of a corner experiences a net forward force.
- A rider braking experiences a net force opposite their direction of travel.

Important teaching point:

Balanced forces do not mean that no forces exist. They mean that the forces cancel when added as vectors.

## 4.6 Interactive Activity — Identify the Forces

Display an illustrated mountain biker traveling on a flat trail.

Ask students to select the forces that may act on the rider-bike system.

Options:

- Gravity.
- Normal force.
- Tire-ground friction.
- Air resistance.
- Spring force.
- Magnetic force.

Students should select and identify the direction of each relevant force.

Provide immediate feedback explaining why each force is or is not applicable to the chosen scenario.

# 5. Section 2 — Newton's First Law: The Law of Inertia

## 5.1 Student-Facing Introduction

Imagine you're riding a trail at 20 km/h.

You stop pedaling.

Do you immediately stop moving?

No.

You continue moving because objects naturally resist changes in their motion.

Newton's First Law explains this behavior.

**Newton's First Law:**

An object remains at rest or continues moving with constant velocity unless acted upon by a nonzero net external force.

This property is called inertia.

Inertia describes an object's resistance to changes in its motion.

Mass is the measure of an object's inertia.

The more mass something has, the more difficult it is to change its motion by the same amount.

## 5.2 Mountain Biking Connections

Teach each concept through an MTB-specific example.

### A. Objects at Rest

A mountain bike sitting at the top of a hill remains stationary until an unbalanced force causes it to move.

**MTB connection:** Your bike doesn't spontaneously roll forward while parked on level ground because the net force is zero.

### B. Constant Velocity

A rider traveling at a constant velocity continues moving at that velocity when the net external force is zero.

**MTB connection:** A rider cruising at a constant speed on flat terrain experiences balanced forward and backward forces.

### C. Inertia

Objects resist changes in their motion.

**MTB connection:** A rider traveling at high speed cannot change their motion instantaneously. The rider and bike have momentum, and forces are needed to alter their velocity.

### D. Mass and Inertia

A more massive system has greater inertia.

**MTB connection:** Under identical net forces, a heavier rider-bike system experiences less acceleration than a lighter system.

### E. Braking and Changes in Motion

An object requires an unbalanced force to slow down.

**MTB connection:** When a rider applies their brakes, tire-ground forces oppose the direction of travel, changing the bike's velocity.

## 5.3 Important Clarification: Why Does a Bike Slow Down When You Stop Pedaling?

This is a particularly important teaching opportunity.

Students often assume that objects require a forward force to keep moving.

Explain:

According to Newton's First Law, an object would continue moving at constant velocity if there were no net external force.

However, a real mountain bike experiences rolling resistance, aerodynamic drag, and other resistive forces.

These forces produce a net force opposite the bike's motion, causing it to slow down.

The bike doesn't need a continuing forward force to maintain motion in an idealized system. It needs a forward force in real riding when that is necessary to balance resistance or accelerate.

## 5.4 Interactive Activity — The Inertia Experiment

Display two mountain bike systems:

- Rider A: 60 kg combined system.
- Rider B: 100 kg combined system.

Apply the same hypothetical horizontal net force to both systems.

Ask:

"Which rider-bike system is harder to accelerate?"

Students select their prediction.

Then reveal that the heavier system has greater inertia and experiences less acceleration under the same net force.

This preview prepares students for Newton's Second Law.

## 5.5 Knowledge Check

**Question 1**

A mountain biker rides along a flat trail at a constant velocity.

What must be true about the net external force acting on the rider-bike system?

A. It must point forward.

B. It must point backward.

C. It must equal zero.

D. Gravity must equal zero.

Correct answer: C.

Explanation: Constant velocity means zero acceleration. According to Newton's First Law, the net external force must therefore be zero.

**Question 2**

Two mountain bikes have different masses. Both experience the same net force.

Which has greater inertia?

A. The lighter bike.

B. The heavier bike.

C. Both have equal inertia.

D. Neither has inertia.

Correct answer: B.

Explanation: Mass measures inertia. A more massive object has greater resistance to changes in motion.

# 6. Section 3 — Newton's Second Law: Force, Mass, and Acceleration

## 6.1 Student-Facing Introduction

Imagine you're climbing a trail and decide to accelerate.

You push harder on the pedals.

Your bike accelerates.

Why?

Newton's Second Law explains how force, mass, and acceleration are related.

**Newton's Second Law:**

The acceleration of an object is determined by its net external force and mass.

The mathematical equation is:

F_net = ma

Where:

- F_net = net external force (N).
- m = mass (kg).
- a = acceleration (m/s²).

This equation is one of the most important relationships in the entire physics course.

## 6.2 The Three Relationships

### A. More Force Means More Acceleration

For a constant mass, increasing net force increases acceleration proportionally.

**MTB connection:** When you push harder on the pedals and increase the net forward force on your bike, you accelerate more quickly.

### B. More Mass Means Less Acceleration

For a constant net force, increasing mass decreases acceleration.

**MTB connection:** A heavier rider-bike system requires a greater net force to achieve the same acceleration as a lighter system.

### C. Acceleration Follows Net Force

The direction of acceleration is the same as the direction of the net force.

**MTB connection:** During braking, the net force acts opposite the bike's direction of travel, producing negative acceleration along the rider's direction of motion.

## 6.3 Guided Mathematical Example

**Scenario: Accelerating Out of a Corner**

A rider and bike have a combined mass of 80 kg.

The net forward force acting on the system is 160 N.

What is the acceleration?

Step 1: Identify the known variables.

m = 80 kg

F_net = 160 N

Step 2: Write the equation.

F_net = ma

Step 3: Rearrange for acceleration.

a = F_net / m

Step 4: Substitute the values.

a = 160 / 80

Step 5: Calculate.

a = 2 m/s²

**Answer:**

The rider-bike system accelerates at 2 m/s².

Interpretation:

Every second, the rider's velocity increases by 2 m/s, assuming constant acceleration.

## 6.4 Applying Newton's Second Law to Braking

**Scenario: Braking Before a Berm**

A rider and bike have a combined mass of 80 kg.

The net force along the trail is 240 N opposite the direction of travel.

Calculate the acceleration along the original direction of motion.

a = -240 / 80

a = -3 m/s²

The negative sign tells us the rider is decelerating relative to the original forward direction.

**MTB connection:** Stronger net braking force creates greater deceleration, assuming the rider-bike mass remains constant.

## 6.5 Applying Newton's Second Law to Climbing

A mountain biker is accelerating uphill.

There are several forces involved:

- Forward force from the tire-ground interaction.
- Gravitational force pulling downhill along the slope.
- Rolling resistance.
- Air resistance.

The forces must be combined to determine net force.

For a simplified straight trail inclined at angle θ:

F_net = F_drive − F_resistance − mg sin(θ)

Then:

a = F_net / m

This allows students to explore how trail steepness affects acceleration.

**MTB connection:** Climbing a steep trail requires the rider to overcome a component of gravity acting against the direction of travel, in addition to resistance.

## 6.6 Interactive Activity — What Makes You Accelerate?

Introduce a slider-based mathematical experiment.

Students adjust:

- Total rider and bike mass.
- Net applied forward force.

Display the resulting acceleration immediately.

Create three experiment challenges:

**Experiment A: Change the Force**

Keep mass constant and increase force.

Students observe the acceleration increase proportionally.

**Experiment B: Change the Mass**

Keep force constant and increase mass.

Students observe acceleration decrease.

**Experiment C: Change Both**

Increase both force and mass.

Students predict the resulting acceleration.

Include a live graph showing:

Acceleration = Net Force / Mass

The graph should update whenever the input values change.

# 7. Section 4 — Newton's Third Law: Action and Reaction

## 7.1 Student-Facing Introduction

Have you ever wondered what actually pushes your mountain bike forward?

You push on the pedals, the drivetrain turns the rear wheel, and the tires interact with the ground.

But how does the ground help push the bike forward?

Newton's Third Law explains how forces occur in pairs.

**Newton's Third Law:**

Whenever one object exerts a force on another object, the second object exerts an equal-magnitude force in the opposite direction on the first object.

These are called action-reaction pairs.

Important:

Action-reaction forces act on different objects. They do not cancel each other on the same free-body diagram.

## 7.2 Mountain Biking Connections

Teach the following examples.

### A. Tire and Ground Interaction

The driven tire pushes backward against the ground.

The ground exerts an opposing forward static friction force on the tire.

That forward force helps propel the bike.

**MTB connection:** The interaction between the tire and trail allows the rider to accelerate forward.

### B. Jump Takeoff

As a rider pushes against the takeoff surface, the bike exerts a force on the ground.

The ground exerts a reaction force on the tires.

**MTB connection:** Tire-ground interaction is part of the force system involved in launching off a jump.

### C. Landing a Jump

When the tires contact the landing surface, they exert a downward force on the ground.

The ground exerts an upward normal force on the tires.

**MTB connection:** The tire-ground interaction changes the rider-bike system's motion during landing.

### D. Braking

When braking, the tire interacts with the ground through friction.

The contact forces between the tire and ground form an action-reaction pair.

**MTB connection:** Braking depends on forces transmitted through the tire-ground interaction.

### E. Pedaling

The rider applies a force to the pedals.

The pedals exert an equal and opposite force back on the rider's feet.

**MTB connection:** Pedaling is an example of interacting forces between the rider and bike components.

## 7.3 Important Misconception

Students often believe action-reaction forces cancel each other.

Explain:

They do not cancel on an individual object's free-body diagram because they act on different objects.

For example:

- The tire pushes backward on the ground.
- The ground pushes forward on the tire.

Both forces are real, but only the second one acts on the tire.

This distinction is essential for correctly analyzing forces.

## 7.4 Interactive Activity — Who Pushes Who?

Display an animated mountain bike tire contacting the trail.

Ask students to identify the action-reaction pair.

Option A:
- Tire pushes backward on ground.
- Ground pushes forward on tire.

Option B:
- Tire pushes forward on ground.
- Ground pushes forward on tire.

Option C:
- Tire pushes downward on ground.
- Ground pushes downward on tire.

Correct answer: A.

Follow up with a landing scenario to demonstrate the vertical force pair.

The animation should visually display two equal-length arrows acting on different objects.

# 8. Section 5 — Free-Body Diagrams

This section connects the three laws into a practical physics skill.

## 8.1 Student-Facing Introduction

We now understand that forces influence motion.

But how do we identify every force acting on a mountain bike?

Physicists use free-body diagrams.

A free-body diagram isolates one object or system and represents all external forces acting on that system.

This makes it easier to calculate net force and determine acceleration.

## 8.2 Common MTB Forces

Introduce each force with a visual example.

| Force | Symbol | MTB connection |
|---|---|---|
| Gravitational force | Fg | Earth's gravity pulls the rider and bike downward. |
| Normal force | FN | The trail pushes perpendicular to the tire-contact surface. |
| Friction | Ff | The tire interacts with the ground during acceleration and braking. |
| Air resistance | Fair | Air opposes the movement of the rider. |
| Spring force | Fs | The suspension spring pushes back against compression. |
| Applied force | Fapp | An external push acts on the rider-bike system. |

Clarify that when the entire rider-bike system is analyzed together, the pedaling forces between the rider and bike are internal forces. The external propulsive force comes from the tire-ground interaction.

## 8.3 Free-Body Diagram — Flat Trail

Display a rider traveling on a flat trail.

For the combined rider-bike system, show:

- Gravitational force downward.
- Normal force upward.
- Forward ground traction when accelerating.
- Backward air resistance and rolling resistance.

At constant speed:

The horizontal forces balance.

The vertical forces balance.

The net force is zero.

When accelerating:

The forward horizontal force exceeds the opposing forces.

When braking:

The resulting horizontal net force points opposite the direction of travel.

## 8.4 Free-Body Diagram — Climbing a Hill

Display a rider climbing an incline.

Show:
- Gravitational force vertically downward.
- Normal force perpendicular to the surface.
- Gravity's component parallel to the slope.
- Gravity's component perpendicular to the slope.
- Tire-ground traction along the trail.
- Resistance forces.

Introduce the gravity components.

Parallel component:

Fg_parallel = mg sin(θ)

Perpendicular component:

Fg_perpendicular = mg cos(θ)

This helps students understand why climbing a steeper trail requires more force to maintain the same motion.

## 8.5 Interactive Activity — Build the Free-Body Diagram

Create an interactive diagram builder.

Students select a mountain biking scenario:

1. Riding on flat ground.
2. Climbing a hill.
3. Braking.
4. Accelerating downhill.

Then they add the correct force vectors to a blank diagram.

The system should:
- Allow students to select force types.
- Display correct force directions.
- Provide labels.
- Explain incorrect selections.
- Calculate net force when appropriate.

For the first version, use selectable force arrows and a guided diagram. A drag-and-drop vector interface can be introduced as a later enhancement.

# 9. Section 6 — Interactive MTB Force Lab

This is the major interactive component of the lesson.

The purpose is to give students an environment where they can experiment with the three laws of motion using a virtual mountain biker.

## 9.1 Simulator Vision

Display a virtual mountain biker traveling along a trail.

Students should be able to modify the forces acting on the rider-bike system and immediately observe changes in motion.

The simulator should have three scenarios:

- Flat Trail.
- Uphill Climb.
- Braking Scenario.

## 9.2 Controls

**Total Rider + Bike Mass**

Range: 45–120 kg.

Default: 80 kg.

**Forward Tire-Ground Traction**

Range: 0–350 N.

Default: 180 N.

This represents the external forward force exerted by the ground on the driven tire in the simplified model.

**Braking Force**

Range: 0–300 N.

Default: 0 N.

**Opposing Resistance**

Range: 0–120 N.

Default: 40 N.

This represents combined rolling resistance and aerodynamic drag in a simplified constant-force approximation.

**Trail Angle**

Range: -10° to +15°.

Default: 0°.

Positive angle represents uphill travel.

Negative angle represents downhill travel.

## 9.3 Physics Calculations

Use the following simplified one-dimensional model.

Positive direction is the rider's direction of travel along the trail.

Gravitational component along the trail:

Fg_parallel = mg sin(θ)

Net force:

F_net = F_traction − F_braking − F_resistance − mg sin(θ)

Acceleration:

a = F_net / m

Normal force magnitude:

N = mg cos(θ)

Assumptions:

- The rider-bike system is treated as a single combined mass.
- The bike travels along a straight trail segment.
- Tire-ground traction is available and the tires do not slip.
- Braking force and resistance are represented as selected magnitudes opposing forward movement.
- The inputs represent a simplified educational scenario, not measured mountain bike performance.

If the net force is negative, display a negative acceleration relative to the original direction of travel.

## 9.4 Simulator Outputs

Display the following values in real time:

- Total mass.
- Forward traction.
- Braking force.
- Opposing resistance.
- Gravity component along the trail.
- Net force.
- Acceleration.
- Normal force.

Provide a visual free-body diagram that updates as students adjust the controls.

The rider animation should visually respond to acceleration.

## 9.5 Three Simulator Experiments

**Experiment 1 — Newton's First Law**

Create a flat-trail configuration where the net force equals zero.

Ask:

"What happens to the rider's velocity?"

Students should identify that velocity remains constant.

Then introduce resistance without additional forward traction.

The rider should begin slowing down.

**Experiment 2 — Newton's Second Law**

Allow students to increase the forward traction force.

Keep mass and resistance constant.

Students observe the resulting acceleration.

Next, increase mass while maintaining the same forces.

Students observe that acceleration decreases.

**Experiment 3 — Newton's Third Law**

Animate a tire contacting the ground.

Display the force the tire applies to the ground and the equal, opposite force the ground applies to the tire.

Allow students to toggle between:
- Force acting on the tire.
- Force acting on the ground.

Explain why these forces do not cancel on the same free-body diagram.

## 9.6 Graphs

Include two essential graphs.

**Graph A — Net Force vs. Acceleration**

Use a fixed mass.

Display the linear relationship:

a = F_net / m

**Graph B — Mass vs. Acceleration**

Use a fixed net force.

Display the inverse relationship:

a = F_net / m

Both graphs must update when relevant controls change.

# 10. Section 7 — Knowledge Check and Final Challenge

The final assessment should determine whether students understand and can apply Newton's laws.

Include a combination of conceptual questions, mathematical calculations, and MTB scenarios.

## 10.1 Assessment Questions

### Question 1 — Newton's First Law

A mountain biker is traveling along a flat trail at a constant velocity.

What is the net force acting on the rider-bike system?

A. It must equal the rider's weight.

B. It must equal zero.

C. It must point forward.

D. It must point backward.

Correct answer: B.

### Question 2 — Inertia

Which system has greater inertia?

A. A 50 kg rider-bike system.

B. A 100 kg rider-bike system.

C. Both have equal inertia.

D. Neither has inertia.

Correct answer: B.

### Question 3 — Newton's Second Law

A rider-bike system has a mass of 75 kg.

The net forward force is 150 N.

Calculate acceleration.

A. 0.5 m/s².

B. 2 m/s².

C. 5 m/s².

D. 11,250 m/s².

Correct answer: B.

Solution:

a = 150 / 75

a = 2 m/s².

### Question 4 — Mass and Acceleration

The net force on a mountain bike doubles while the mass remains constant.

What happens to acceleration?

A. It doubles.

B. It halves.

C. It quadruples.

D. It remains the same.

Correct answer: A.

### Question 5 — Newton's Third Law

When a driven tire pushes backward against the ground, what does the ground do?

A. Nothing.

B. Pushes forward against the tire.

C. Pushes backward against the tire.

D. Reduces the bike's mass.

Correct answer: B.

### Question 6 — Free-Body Diagrams

A rider is traveling on a flat trail at constant speed.

Which statement about the forces is correct?

A. There are no forces acting.

B. Vertical forces and horizontal forces each balance.

C. Gravity disappears.

D. The normal force is greater than gravity.

Correct answer: B.

### Question 7 — Weight

A rider and bike have a combined mass of 80 kg.

Calculate their weight.

W = mg

W = 80 × 9.81

W = 784.8 N.

### Question 8 — Applied Problem

A rider and bike have a combined mass of 75 kg.

They are accelerating uphill on a 5-degree slope.

The forward tire-ground traction force is 240 N.

The opposing resistance is 45 N.

Calculate the acceleration.

Given:

m = 75 kg

F_traction = 240 N

F_resistance = 45 N

θ = 5°

Step 1: Calculate the gravitational force component along the slope.

Fg_parallel = mg sin(θ)

Fg_parallel = 75 × 9.81 × sin(5°)

Fg_parallel ≈ 64.1 N

Step 2: Calculate net force.

F_net = 240 − 45 − 64.1

F_net ≈ 130.9 N

Step 3: Calculate acceleration.

a = 130.9 / 75

a ≈ 1.75 m/s²

Correct answer: Approximately 1.75 m/s² uphill.

The question should allow students to submit their working, not simply select a numerical answer.

## 10.2 Final Challenge — The Climbing Trail

**Challenge title:** Can You Accelerate Uphill?

Scenario:

You are riding a mountain bike up a steep trail.

Your combined rider and bike mass is 85 kg.

The trail is inclined at 8 degrees.

Your tire-ground traction force is 300 N.

The opposing resistance is 55 N.

Your objective is to calculate the resulting acceleration.

Students must:

1. Identify the relevant forces.
2. Draw or select a free-body diagram.
3. Calculate the gravitational force component.
4. Calculate net force.
5. Apply Newton's Second Law.
6. Explain what the acceleration means for the rider.

The simulation should allow students to input their calculated values and verify the result.

Use the simplified model defined in the MTB Force Lab.

# 11. Lesson Differentiation

Provide three levels of learning support.

## Explore — Foundational

For students who are new to physics.

- Visual demonstrations.
- Simple MTB examples.
- Minimal equation manipulation.
- Guided questions.
- Basic force diagrams.
- Vocabulary explanations.

## Apply — Standard

For students studying high-school physics.

- All three Newton's laws.
- Force calculations.
- Mass and acceleration problems.
- Free-body diagrams.
- Inclined-plane calculations.
- Multi-step mathematical problems.

## Engineer — Advanced

For students who want greater mathematical depth.

- Two-dimensional force analysis.
- Vector decomposition.
- Friction models.
- Multiple simultaneous forces.
- Comparing different rider masses.
- Evaluating model assumptions.
- Designing their own force experiments.

Students should be able to access different levels of support without changing the underlying lesson content.

# 18. Definition of Success

A student should be able to finish this lesson and look at a mountain bike riding down a trail and recognize the physics happening around them.

They should understand:

- Why the bike keeps moving.
- Why it accelerates.
- Why mass matters.
- Why the ground pushes back on the tires.
- Why the rider slows down when braking.
- Why different forces act on the bike.
- How to calculate acceleration from net force.

Most importantly, they should be able to explain their reasoning rather than simply repeat an equation.

**The Mathlete MTB standard:**

Don't just teach students what Newton's laws are.

Teach them to recognize Newton's laws in the world around them, investigate them, and use mathematics to explain what they observe.
