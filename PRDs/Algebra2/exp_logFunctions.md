# 1. PRODUCT VISION

## The Big Idea

Create an interactive mountain bike engineering experience where students learn exponential and logarithmic functions through suspension dynamics, event growth, and sound analysis.

The student becomes a mountain bike systems engineer.

Their job is to investigate three different systems:

1. How suspension damping reduces the amplitude of repeated oscillations after a bump.
2. How participation at a mountain bike event can grow by a constant percentage over time.
3. How sound intensity at a mountain bike event is measured on a logarithmic scale.

Students will:
- Understand exponential growth and decay.
- Recognize geometric sequences.
- Construct exponential functions from data.
- Compare linear and exponential models.
- Understand the natural base e.
- Evaluate exponential functions.
- Understand logarithms as the inverse of exponents.
- Graph logarithmic functions.
- Apply logarithm properties.
- Use change of base.
- Solve exponential equations with logarithms.
- Solve logarithmic equations.
- Interpret logarithmic scales in a real-world context.

The lesson should feel like a combination of a professional suspension engineering lab, mountain bike event planning dashboard, and technical sound-analysis workstation.

## Core Educational Philosophy

Mountain biking contains systems that change over time and relationships that span enormous ranges of values.

Exponential functions help describe quantities that change by a consistent percentage or factor.

Logarithms help us work backward to determine the time, exponent, or input required to produce a particular result.

The student should discover that these aren't arbitrary mathematical concepts.

They are tools that help engineers and analysts understand how systems behave.

**The goal: Make exponential and logarithmic mathematics feel like mountain bike engineering.**

## Important Modeling Distinctions

Several scenarios in this lesson use simplified mathematical models.

The suspension simulation models the exponential decay envelope of successive oscillation peaks. It is not a complete physical model of an actual mountain bike shock.

The event registration scenario uses fictional growth data over a limited planning period. It is not a prediction of unlimited real-world event growth.

The sound analysis scenario uses the mathematical definition of sound intensity level. It is not a safety assessment of actual event noise.

Make these distinctions clear to students.

# 4. MISSION 1 — TUNE THE BOUNCE

**Mathematical Focus:** Exponential decay, geometric sequences, exponential functions, transformations, the natural base e, and solving exponential equations using logarithms.

**Estimated Completion:** 7–8 minutes.

This should be the signature experience of the entire lesson.

## The Scenario

"You've just ridden a section of technical trail with a series of bumps.

Your bike's suspension compresses and rebounds after each impact.

The suspension damper controls how quickly the shock returns after compression and helps manage the spring's stored energy.

Your engineering team wants to model how the remaining oscillation amplitude decreases over time.

Your job is to investigate the relationship mathematically."

Introduce:
- Spring.
- Damper.
- Compression.
- Rebound.
- Oscillation.
- Amplitude.
- Exponential decay.

FOX describes rebound as controlling how quickly a shock extends after compression. RockShox explains that damping controls suspension compression and rebound speed.

Use these concepts to establish an authentic mountain bike connection.

## Important Physical Modeling Explanation

An underdamped spring-and-damper system can exhibit oscillations whose amplitude decreases according to an exponential envelope.

The complete physical motion also contains an oscillating component.

For this Algebra 2 activity, do not require students to understand trigonometric oscillation equations.

Instead, focus on the sequence of successive peak amplitudes and the exponential envelope containing those peaks.

Clearly state that this is a simplified spring-and-damper model.

## The Mathematical Model

Use:

\[
A_n=A_0r^n
\]

Where:
- A₀ is the initial displacement amplitude.
- r is the decay factor per oscillation cycle.
- n is the number of cycles.
- Aₙ is the remaining peak amplitude.

Use fictional simulation values:

\[
A_0=18\text{ mm}
\]

\[
r=0.65
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{A_n=18(0.65)^n}
\]

Interpretation:

The modeled peak amplitude retains 65% of its previous value after each cycle.

It therefore decreases by 35% per cycle.

These values are educational simulation assumptions, not recommended suspension settings or measured specifications for a real shock.

### Example Table

| Cycle | Remaining amplitude |
|---:|---:|
| 0 | 18 mm |
| 1 | 11.7 mm |
| 2 | 7.605 mm |
| 3 | 4.943 mm |
| 4 | 3.213 mm |
| 5 | 2.089 mm |
| 6 | 1.358 mm |

Explain why the differences between successive amplitudes are not constant.

The relationship is exponential because the amplitude changes by a constant factor.

## Learning Module A — Exponential Versus Linear Change

Ask:

"If the suspension retained the same number of millimeters of movement after every cycle, would that describe the same pattern?"

Introduce two models.

Exponential:

\[
A_n=18(0.65)^n
\]

Linear comparison:

\[
L_n=18-6.3n
\]

Both models begin at 18 mm and have the same first-cycle value.

However, the exponential model multiplies the remaining amplitude by 0.65 every cycle.

The linear model subtracts a constant amount each cycle.

Display both tables and graphs.

Ask students to identify:
- Which model changes by equal differences?
- Which model changes by equal factors?
- Which model better represents the chosen fixed-percentage decay scenario?

Explain that the linear model would eventually become negative, so it is not an appropriate long-term model of a nonnegative amplitude envelope.

The displayed mathematical curve should represent the upper and lower exponential envelopes, not claim to simulate every physical detail of a real shock.

| Retained fraction | 65% |

## Learning Module B — Geometric Sequences

Explain:

"The sequence of amplitudes forms a geometric sequence because every term is found by multiplying the previous term by the same common ratio."

For this model:

\[
18,\ 11.7,\ 7.605,\ 4.943,\ldots
\]

Common ratio:

\[
r=0.65
\]

Explicit formula:

\[
A_n=18(0.65)^n
\]

Recursive formula:

\[
A_0=18
\]

\[
A_n=0.65A_{n-1}
\]

Ask the student to identify:
- First term.
- Common ratio.
- Decay percentage.
- Sixth term.

### Interactive Challenge

Give a different initial amplitude and retention percentage.

Ask the student to construct the explicit formula.

Allow them to toggle between recursive and explicit forms.

Show how the two descriptions generate the same sequence.

## Learning Module C — Transformations of Exponential Functions

Introduce:

\[
f(x)=b^x
\]

Then:

\[
g(x)=a b^{x-h}+k
\]

Explain:
- a controls vertical scaling and reflection.
- b determines growth or decay when b is positive and not equal to 1.
- h produces a horizontal shift.
- k produces a vertical shift and changes the horizontal asymptote.

Use the suspension envelope as the primary example.

Allow students to adjust the initial amplitude and decay rate.

Show:
- Increasing initial amplitude moves the curve upward.
- Increasing the decay factor toward 1 makes decay slower.
- Decreasing the decay factor makes decay faster.
- The horizontal asymptote is y = 0 for the basic amplitude model.

Keep the physical interpretation and the mathematical transformations distinct.

## Learning Module D — Introduce the Natural Base e

Move from the discrete cycle model to a continuous exponential envelope.

Using a 0.5-second interval between modeled peaks and a 0.65 retained fraction per cycle:

\[
k=-\frac{\ln(0.65)}{0.5}
\]

\[
k\approx0.8616
\]

The continuous exponential envelope becomes:

\[
\boxed{A(t)=18e^{-0.8616t}}
\]

Where:
- t is time in seconds.
- A(t) is the modeled amplitude envelope in millimeters.
- e is Euler's number.

Explain that the continuous model and discrete sequence describe the same chosen decay behavior at corresponding half-second intervals.

Do not introduce differential equations.

Explain e as the natural exponential base that appears in continuous growth and decay models.

### Interactive Component: Cycle Versus Time

Display two connected views:

**Discrete View**
- Cycle number.
- Peak amplitude.
- Geometric sequence.

**Continuous View**
- Time in seconds.
- Exponential envelope.
- Corresponding peak locations.

Allow students to move between the two representations.

## Learning Module E — Solve for the Settling Time Using Logarithms

Introduce a modeled settling threshold of 2 mm.

Ask:

"How long does it take for the amplitude envelope to fall below 2 mm?"

Start with:

\[
18e^{-0.8616t}=2
\]

Divide:

\[
e^{-0.8616t}=\frac{2}{18}
\]

Take the natural logarithm:

\[
-0.8616t=\ln\left(\frac{1}{9}\right)
\]

Solve:

\[
t=\frac{\ln(1/9)}{-0.8616}
\]

\[
\boxed{t\approx2.55\text{ seconds}}
\]

Explain how natural logarithms allow students to solve for an unknown exponent.

Also connect the discrete version:

\[
18(0.65)^n=2
\]

\[
n=\frac{\ln(2/18)}{\ln(0.65)}
\]

\[
n\approx5.10
\]

The envelope reaches the threshold after approximately 5.10 cycles, meaning the first complete peak below the threshold is at cycle 6.

### Interactive Component: Settling Time Calculator

Allow students to:
- Change the initial amplitude.
- Change the decay rate.
- Change the threshold.
- Calculate time or cycle count.

Display the corresponding equation and logarithmic solution.

Show a marker at the threshold crossing.

Make clear that the threshold is an arbitrary educational measurement, not a real suspension tuning specification.

# 5. MISSION 2 — GROW THE RIDE

**Mathematical Focus:** Exponential growth, geometric sequences, exponential models, transformations, comparison with linear growth, and solving exponential equations.

**Estimated Completion:** 5–6 minutes.

## The Scenario

"A local mountain bike event has announced a new trail festival.

During the first week of registration, 100 riders sign up.

The event's promotional campaign is attracting new riders, and the team estimates that registrations will grow by 20% each week over the next six weeks.

Your job is to forecast attendance and understand the mathematics behind this growth."

Explicitly describe this as fictional simulated registration data.

The fixed percentage is an illustrative short-term model, not a claim that actual event attendance follows an exponential pattern indefinitely.

## The Mathematical Model

Let:
- w = number of weeks since the campaign began.
- R(w) = projected cumulative registrations.

Initial registration count:

\[
R_0=100
\]

Weekly growth factor:

\[
1+0.20=1.20
\]

Exponential model:

\[
\boxed{R(w)=100(1.20)^w}
\]

Limit the model to:

\[
0\leq w\leq6
\]

The event has a practical capacity of 300 riders, so the model should not be extrapolated beyond its limited planning window.

### Example Table

| Week | Projected registrations |
|---:|---:|
| 0 | 100 |
| 1 | 120 |
| 2 | 144 |
| 3 | 173 |
| 4 | 207 |
| 5 | 249 |
| 6 | 299 |

Round projected registration counts to whole riders for display.

Keep full precision internally for calculations.

## Learning Module A — Identify Exponential Growth

Explain:

"Exponential growth happens when a quantity changes by the same percentage or multiplication factor over equal time intervals."

Ask the student:

"What is the growth factor?"

Answer:

\[
1.20
\]

"What percentage growth does this represent?"

Answer:

20%.

Show the relationship between:
- Initial value.
- Growth factor.
- Exponent.
- Number of time intervals.

### Interactive Component: Event Growth Simulator

Display:
- A mountain bike event registration dashboard.
- Projected rider sign-ups.
- A progress bar toward the 300-rider capacity.
- An exponential graph.
- A weekly registration table.

Controls:
- Initial registrations.
- Weekly growth percentage.
- Number of weeks.

Update the equation and graph whenever the student changes an input.

Show the difference between:
- Increase by 20% per week.
- Increase by 20 riders per week.

## Learning Module B — Exponential Versus Linear Growth

Build two models:

Exponential:

\[
R(w)=100(1.20)^w
\]

Linear:

\[
L(w)=100+20w
\]

Display both graphs.

Explain:
- The linear model adds the same number every week.
- The exponential model multiplies by the same factor every week.

Ask:

"Which model predicts more registrations by week 6?"

Exponential:

\[
R(6)\approx299
\]

Linear:

\[
L(6)=220
\]

The exponential model predicts more registrations.

Explain that exponential growth eventually outpaces linear and polynomial growth, but the real event cannot exceed its capacity.

## Learning Module C — Solve an Exponential Growth Equation

The event needs 250 projected registrations to qualify for an additional race category.

Ask:

"How many weeks will it take to reach 250 registrations?"

Construct:

\[
100(1.20)^w=250
\]

Divide:

\[
1.20^w=2.5
\]

Take logarithms:

\[
w\ln(1.20)=\ln(2.5)
\]

Solve:

\[
w=\frac{\ln(2.5)}{\ln(1.20)}
\]

\[
\boxed{w\approx5.03}
\]

Interpretation:

The continuous model predicts reaching 250 registrations approximately 5.03 weeks after registration begins.

At weekly check-ins, the model first reaches or exceeds 250 during week 6.

### Interactive Component: Growth Target Calculator

Allow the student to enter:
- Initial registrations.
- Growth percentage.
- Target number of registrations.

Calculate the time needed using logarithms.

Display:
- Exponential equation.
- Logarithmic conversion.
- Calculated time.
- Projected number at the next complete week.

## Optional Learning Module D — Finite Geometric Series

Introduce an optional trail festival fundraising scenario.

A sponsor contributes a modeled amount that grows by 20% each week:

\[
100,\ 120,\ 144,\ 172.8,\ 207.36
\]

Explain that this is a geometric sequence.

The total contribution over the first five periods is:

\[
S_5=100+120+144+172.8+207.36
\]

Use the finite geometric series formula:

\[
S_n=a\frac{r^n-1}{r-1}
\]

For this example:

\[
S_5=100\frac{1.2^5-1}{0.2}
\]

\[
\boxed{S_5=744.16}
\]

Explain what the series represents: adding several geometrically changing values together.

Use this as an optional extension depending on the school's Algebra 2 sequence.

# 6. MISSION 3 — DECODE THE SOUND

**Mathematical Focus:** Logarithmic functions, common logarithms, inverse relationships, logarithm evaluation, logarithm properties, change of base, and solving logarithmic equations.

**Estimated Completion:** 6–7 minutes.

## The Scenario

"The mountain bike festival has a pump track competition, a race announcer, spectators, and trail maintenance activity.

The event team is collecting sound intensity readings from different areas of the venue.

But sound intensity can span an enormous range of values.

Instead of displaying raw intensity, the sound-analysis system uses decibels.

Your job is to figure out how logarithms make this measurement system work."

Explain that decibels use a logarithmic scale.

Distinguish sound intensity from sound intensity level.

## The Mathematical Model

Use the sound intensity level definition:

\[
\boxed{
L=10\log_{10}\left(\frac{I}{I_0}\right)
}
\]

Where:
- L = sound intensity level in decibels.
- I = sound intensity in watts per square meter.
- I₀ = reference intensity.

Use:

\[
I_0=10^{-12}\text{ W/m}^2
\]

This is the reference sound intensity used in the educational model.

All example readings are fictional.

### Example Data

| Example event location | Sound intensity | Intensity level |
|---|---:|---:|
| Quiet trail zone | \(10^{-8}\) W/m² | 40 dB |
| Warm-up area | \(10^{-7}\) W/m² | 50 dB |
| Pump track competition | \(10^{-5}\) W/m² | 70 dB |
| Event PA area | \(10^{-4}\) W/m² | 80 dB |

Use these as simulated values, not actual measurements of those locations.

## Learning Module A — Why Use Logarithms?

Explain:

"A logarithm tells us what exponent is needed to produce a certain number."

For example:

\[
10^3=1000
\]

Therefore:

\[
\log_{10}(1000)=3
\]

A logarithmic scale allows us to represent very large ratios in a compact way.

Introduce:

\[
L=10\log_{10}\left(\frac{I}{I_0}\right)
\]

For an intensity of:

\[
I=10^{-5}
\]

The ratio is:

\[
\frac{10^{-5}}{10^{-12}}=10^7
\]

Therefore:

\[
L=10\log_{10}(10^7)
\]

\[
L=70\text{ dB}
\]

Explain that every tenfold increase in sound intensity corresponds to a 10 dB increase in sound intensity level.

A decibel difference does not represent a simple equal-percentage increase in intensity.

## Interactive Component: Decibel Analyzer

Create an interactive sound-intensity analysis dashboard.

Visual elements:
- Fictional pump track event scene.
- Sound-source icons.
- Sound-intensity meter.
- Digital decibel readout.
- Logarithmic graph.

Controls:
- Adjust sound intensity.
- Select a reference location.
- Compare two sound sources.
- Convert between intensity and decibels.

When intensity changes:
- The sound meter changes.
- The logarithmic equation updates.
- The decibel output updates.
- The graph marker moves.

Do not simulate or claim to measure actual sound.

## Learning Module B — Logarithmic and Exponential Forms

Start with:

\[
L=10\log_{10}\left(\frac{I}{I_0}\right)
\]

Rearrange:

\[
\frac{L}{10}=\log_{10}\left(\frac{I}{I_0}\right)
\]

Convert to exponential form:

\[
10^{L/10}=\frac{I}{I_0}
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{I=I_0 10^{L/10}}
\]

Explain that exponentials and logarithms are inverse operations.

### Interactive Conversion Tool

Allow students to enter:
- An exponential equation.
- A logarithmic equation.

Display its equivalent form.

Example:

\[
10^4=10000
\]

Equivalent:

\[
\log_{10}(10000)=4
\]

Then connect the conversion back to the sound-intensity model.

## Learning Module C — Understanding Logarithm Properties

Use the sound intensity ratios to introduce the properties.

### Product Property

\[
\log_b(MN)=\log_bM+\log_bN
\]

Explain how multiplying two ratios allows their logarithms to be added.

### Quotient Property

\[
\log_b\left(\frac{M}{N}\right)
=
\log_bM-\log_bN
\]

Explain how dividing ratios allows their logarithms to be subtracted.

### Power Property

\[
\log_b(M^p)=p\log_bM
\]

Show how powers become multiplication outside the logarithm.

Use numerical sound-intensity ratios to demonstrate the properties.

For example:

\[
\log_{10}(100\times1000)
\]

\[
=\log_{10}(100)+\log_{10}(1000)
\]

\[
=2+3=5
\]

### Important Sound-Level Distinction

When two independent sound sources operate simultaneously, their physical intensities add before converting to a decibel level.

Do not add their decibel readings directly.

Include an optional example demonstrating how two equal intensity sources combine.

## Learning Module D — Common Logarithms and Change of Base

Introduce:
- Common logarithm: base 10.
- Natural logarithm: base e.

Explain:

\[
\log(x)=\log_{10}(x)
\]

and:

\[
\ln(x)=\log_e(x)
\]

Introduce the change of base formula:

\[
\log_b(x)=\frac{\ln(x)}{\ln(b)}
\]

Allow students to evaluate logarithms with different bases using a calculator-style interface.

Example:

\[
\log_2(32)=5
\]

For a non-integer example:

\[
\log_3(20)=\frac{\ln(20)}{\ln(3)}
\]

Display the approximate numerical result.

Explain why change of base allows technology to calculate logarithms using common or natural logs even when a calculator does not have a button for an arbitrary base.

## Learning Module E — Solve a Logarithmic Equation

The event system records a sound intensity level of 75 dB.

Ask:

"What sound intensity corresponds to this reading?"

Start with:

\[
75=10\log_{10}\left(\frac{I}{10^{-12}}\right)
\]

Divide by 10:

\[
7.5=\log_{10}\left(\frac{I}{10^{-12}}\right)
\]

Convert to exponential form:

\[
10^{7.5}=\frac{I}{10^{-12}}
\]

Solve:

\[
I=10^{-12}\times10^{7.5}
\]

\[
\boxed{
I\approx3.1623\times10^{-5}\text{ W/m}^2
}
\]

Show the solution and the sound-meter reading.

### Independent Challenge

Give the student a different decibel reading and ask them to calculate the corresponding sound intensity.

Provide appropriate numerical precision.

## Challenge A — Suspension Decay

A different simulated suspension envelope begins at 24 mm and retains 70% of its amplitude per modeled cycle.

Ask:

1. Write the exponential function.
2. Identify the initial value.
3. Identify the decay factor.
4. Calculate the amplitude after four cycles.
5. Determine how many complete cycles are needed for the amplitude to fall below 3 mm.

Function:

\[
A(n)=24(0.70)^n
\]

After four cycles:

\[
A(4)=24(0.70)^4
\]

\[
A(4)=5.7624\text{ mm}
\]

For the threshold:

\[
24(0.70)^n\leq3
\]

\[
0.70^n\leq0.125
\]

\[
n\geq\frac{\ln(0.125)}{\ln(0.70)}
\]

\[
n\geq5.83
\]

The first complete cycle below the threshold is cycle 6.

## Challenge B — Event Growth

A mountain bike festival starts with 150 registrations.

Projected registrations grow by 15% each week.

Ask:

1. Construct the exponential model.
2. Calculate registrations after four weeks.
3. Find when projected registrations reach 250.
4. Compare the result with a linear model that adds 22.5 riders per week.

Function:

\[
R(w)=150(1.15)^w
\]

After four weeks:

\[
R(4)\approx262.35
\]

For 250 registrations:

\[
150(1.15)^w=250
\]

\[
w=\frac{\ln(250/150)}{\ln(1.15)}
\]

\[
w\approx3.61
\]

Interpretation:

The continuous model predicts reaching 250 registrations approximately 3.61 weeks after the campaign begins.

For weekly discrete check-ins, the target is first reached in week 4.

## Challenge C — Sound Intensity

A fictional race event sound sensor records an intensity level of 80 dB.

Ask students to:
1. Write the logarithmic equation.
2. Convert it into exponential form.
3. Calculate the intensity.
4. Explain what a 10 dB increase represents in terms of intensity.

\[
80=10\log_{10}\left(\frac{I}{10^{-12}}\right)
\]

Expected result:

\[
I=10^{-4}\text{ W/m}^2
\]

A 10 dB increase corresponds to ten times the sound intensity.

## Challenge D — Reverse the Suspension Model Using Natural Logs

A shock's modeled oscillation amplitude follows:

\[
A(t)=24e^{-0.9t}
\]

Ask:

"How long does it take for the envelope to decrease to 4 mm?"

Construct:

\[
4=24e^{-0.9t}
\]

\[
\frac16=e^{-0.9t}
\]

Take natural logarithms:

\[
\ln\left(\frac16\right)=-0.9t
\]

\[
t=\frac{\ln(1/6)}{-0.9}
\]

\[
\boxed{t\approx1.99\text{ seconds}}
\]

Interpret the answer and explain why logarithms are required.

The Suspension & Systems Lab should demonstrate that exponential and logarithmic functions describe real relationships that change by factors, percentages, and orders of magnitude.

Students begin by investigating how suspension movement diminishes after an impact.

They then explore exponential growth through a mountain bike event and learn how logarithms can calculate the time required to reach a target.

Finally, they investigate sound intensity and discover why logarithms are used to measure quantities that span enormous ranges.

The student should finish thinking:

"I understand how exponential growth and decay work, and I can use logarithms to solve problems involving real mountain bike systems."
