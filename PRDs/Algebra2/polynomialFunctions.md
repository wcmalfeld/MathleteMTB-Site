# 1. PRODUCT VISION

## The Big Idea

Build an interactive pump track design and analysis experience where students learn polynomial functions by investigating the shapes of mountain bike trails.

The student becomes a pump track profile engineer.

Their assignment is to analyze and redesign a section of a fictional pump track containing:
- Rollers.
- Dips.
- Transitions.
- Raised sections.
- Successive waves.

They need to understand how polynomial functions describe these different features and how algebra can be used to investigate the underlying profile.

Students will:
- Analyze polynomial graphs.
- Identify degree and leading coefficients.
- Understand polynomial end behavior.
- Add, subtract, and multiply polynomials.
- Build polynomial functions from factors.
- Find zeros and understand multiplicity.
- Factor higher-degree polynomials.
- Use the Rational Zero Theorem.
- Use synthetic division.
- Apply the Remainder and Factor Theorems.
- Interpret polynomial division.

## Core Philosophy

A pump track is built around rhythm, transitions, rollers, and berms.

Its side profile can be represented mathematically using curves.

Polynomial functions provide a way to model complex curves with multiple peaks, dips, and transitions.

Students should discover that the structure of a polynomial equation reveals important information about the shape of its graph.

For example:
- Degree tells us the highest power of the function.
- Leading coefficients and degree determine end behavior.
- Factors reveal the zeros.
- Multiplicity determines how the graph behaves around a zero.
- Polynomial division helps us uncover unknown factors.
- The Remainder Theorem helps us test potential zeros.

**The goal: Help students understand how to read, build, and reverse-engineer polynomial functions through a mountain biking scenario.**

## Important Modeling Distinction

A polynomial curve is a simplified mathematical representation of a trail's vertical profile over a defined distance.

It is not a complete pump track engineering design.

Real pump tracks are continuous circuits with successive rollers and berms. Their dimensions and geometry depend on the site, intended riders, and professional design considerations.

Use the polynomial models as educational illustrations, not instructions for constructing actual riding features.

# 4. MISSION 1 — THE FLOW LINE DECODER

**Mathematical Focus:** Polynomial definitions, degree, leading coefficient, end behavior, turning points, and polynomial graph interpretation.

**Estimated Completion:** 5–6 minutes.

## The Scenario

"Your pump track development team has mapped a new section of terrain.

The elevation profile includes several rises and dips.

The crew wants to understand the shape of this section before moving forward.

You've received a mathematical model of the track, but you need to figure out what the equation tells you."

Introduce:
- Polynomial functions.
- Degree.
- Leading coefficient.
- End behavior.
- Turning points.

## The Trail Model

Create a fictional pump track segment called:

**Flow Line A**

Define:

- x = horizontal distance along the modeled track in meters.
- P(x) = vertical height relative to a chosen reference elevation in meters.

Use:

\[
P(x)=0.005x(x-2)(x-6)(x-8)
\]

Restrict the actual modeled section to:

\[
0\leq x\leq8
\]

This is a simulated mathematical side profile, not a construction specification.

### Expanded Form

Show the equivalent expression:

\[
P(x)=0.005x^4-0.08x^3+0.38x^2-0.48x
\]

Explain that these two equations represent exactly the same polynomial function.

### Important Graph Features

Zeros:

\[
x=0,\ 2,\ 6,\ 8
\]

Degree:

\[
4
\]

Leading coefficient:

\[
0.005
\]

Leading term:

\[
0.005x^4
\]

The vertical reference line is:

\[
P(x)=0
\]

The profile:
- Begins at the reference elevation.
- Dips below the reference line.
- Rises above the reference line.
- Dips again.
- Returns to the reference elevation.

Display these features in a visually engaging interactive graph.

## Learning Module A — What Is a Polynomial?

Explain:

"A polynomial is an expression made up of terms with variables raised to nonnegative integer powers, multiplied by coefficients."

Examples:

\[
3x^2+4x+1
\]

\[
x^3-5x+6
\]

\[
2x^4-3x^2+7
\]

Non-examples:

\[
\frac{1}{x}+2
\]

\[
\sqrt{x}+4
\]

\[
2^x+1
\]

Explain why the non-examples are not polynomial functions of x.

Connect this to the idea that polynomial functions have smooth, continuous graphs with no sharp corners or breaks.

## Learning Module B — Degree and Leading Coefficient

Highlight the expanded equation.

\[
P(x)=0.005x^4-0.08x^3+0.38x^2-0.48x
\]

Ask the student:
- What is the highest exponent?
- What is the degree?
- What is the leading term?
- What is the leading coefficient?

Expected answers:
- Degree: 4.
- Leading term: \(0.005x^4\).
- Leading coefficient: 0.005.

Explain that the degree and leading coefficient give us important information about the overall mathematical behavior of a polynomial graph.

## Learning Module C — End Behavior

Introduce the connection between degree and leading coefficient.

For a polynomial:
- Even degree and positive leading coefficient: both ends rise.
- Even degree and negative leading coefficient: both ends fall.
- Odd degree and positive leading coefficient: left falls, right rises.
- Odd degree and negative leading coefficient: left rises, right falls.

For Flow Line A:

\[
P(x)=0.005x^4+\ldots
\]

The degree is even and the leading coefficient is positive.

Therefore:

As x approaches negative infinity, P(x) approaches positive infinity.

As x approaches positive infinity, P(x) approaches positive infinity.

### Important Contextual Distinction

Our actual modeled pump track exists only on:

\[
0\leq x\leq8
\]

End behavior describes the mathematical polynomial if it is extended beyond that interval.

It does not mean the actual pump track continues upward forever.

Make this distinction explicit.

### Interactive Component: Polynomial End Behavior Explorer

Create four selectable polynomial examples.

Students should be able to:
- Change the degree.
- Change the sign of the leading coefficient.
- See how the graph changes.
- Predict end behavior.
- Receive feedback.

Use simple parent polynomial functions for this demonstration.

## Learning Module D — Turning Points

Explain:

"A turning point occurs when a graph changes direction."

A polynomial of degree n has at most n - 1 turning points.

For a fourth-degree polynomial:

\[
4-1=3
\]

Therefore, it can have at most three turning points.

Show the Flow Line A graph.

Allow students to identify the three turning points by visually selecting them.

Do not require calculus or derivative calculations.

Ask:

"Could a fourth-degree polynomial have five turning points?"

Expected answer: No.

Explain why the degree places a limit on the maximum number of turning points.

## Interactive Component: Flow Line Decoder

Display:
- The terrain illustration.
- The polynomial equation.
- The coordinate plane.
- A control panel showing degree and leading coefficient.

Allow students to toggle between:
- Standard form.
- Factored form.
- Graph view.
- Feature identification.

The graph and equation must always describe the same function.

# 5. MISSION 2 — SHAPE THE TRAIL

**Mathematical Focus:** Polynomial addition, subtraction, multiplication, expansion, and factorized polynomial models.

**Estimated Completion:** 5–6 minutes.

## The Scenario

"Your development team is combining an existing terrain profile with a proposed shaping adjustment.

You need to determine what the resulting mathematical profile looks like.

Then, the crew gives you a factored equation and asks you to expand it to reveal the complete polynomial."

## Learning Module A — Add and Subtract Polynomial Profiles

Introduce two simulated functions.

### Existing Terrain Profile

\[
B(x)=0.02x^2+0.1x+1
\]

### Proposed Shaping Adjustment

\[
A(x)=-0.005x^3+0.03x^2-0.1x
\]

Both represent vertical values in meters relative to the chosen model datum, over the same illustrative distance interval.

Explain:

- B(x) is the baseline mathematical profile.
- A(x) is a modeled vertical adjustment.
- The combined profile is the sum of the two functions.

Construct:

\[
T(x)=B(x)+A(x)
\]

Substitute:

\[
T(x)=
(0.02x^2+0.1x+1)
+
(-0.005x^3+0.03x^2-0.1x)
\]

Combine like terms:

\[
T(x)=-0.005x^3+0.05x^2+1
\]

Explain that the result is a third-degree polynomial.

### Interactive Component: Trail Profile Combination Studio

Display:
- Baseline profile.
- Shaping adjustment.
- Combined result.

Students should be able to toggle each graph independently.

Use sliders to change selected coefficients in a practice mode.

Show the combined equation updating automatically.

Ask:

"What happens to the x-term when we combine these two functions?"

Expected answer: The linear terms cancel because 0.1x and -0.1x add to zero.

### Polynomial Subtraction

Reverse the operation:

\[
T(x)-B(x)=A(x)
\]

Explain how subtraction can recover the modeled adjustment from the combined profile and baseline.

Include a short subtraction exercise where students must correctly distribute the negative sign and combine like terms.

## Learning Module B — Multiply Polynomials to Build a Profile

Return to Flow Line A.

\[
P(x)=0.005x(x-2)(x-6)(x-8)
\]

Explain:

"Sometimes we know where a profile crosses the reference elevation, but we want to expand the equation so we can study its complete polynomial structure."

Demonstrate polynomial multiplication.

Group the factors:

\[
x(x-8)=x^2-8x
\]

And:

\[
(x-2)(x-6)=x^2-8x+12
\]

Multiply:

\[
(x^2-8x)(x^2-8x+12)
\]

Simplify:

\[
x^4-16x^3+76x^2-96x
\]

Multiply by 0.005:

\[
P(x)=0.005x^4-0.08x^3+0.38x^2-0.48x
\]

Now the student can see the connection between factored form and standard form.

### Interactive Component: Polynomial Expansion Workbench

Create a polynomial multiplication workspace.

Students should be able to:
- Select two polynomial factors.
- Multiply individual terms.
- Drag or select matching terms.
- Combine like terms.
- Reveal the simplified expression.
- Observe how the resulting polynomial degree changes.

Use a visual area-model or multiplication-grid approach.

For the Flow Line A example, show how the two quadratic expressions combine to create a fourth-degree polynomial.

### Learning Module C — Degree Rules

Explain the relationships:

- Adding polynomials may produce a result with the same or a lower degree if leading terms cancel.
- Subtracting polynomials follows the same principle.
- Multiplying polynomials adds their degrees, provided the leading coefficients are nonzero.

Use examples from the profile models.

### Challenge

Give students a new expression:

\[
(x^2+2x)(x^2-3x+1)
\]

Ask them to expand it.

Expected result:

\[
x^4-x^3-5x^2+2x
\]

Have them identify the resulting degree and leading coefficient.

# 6. MISSION 3 — FIND THE HIDDEN FEATURES

**Mathematical Focus:** Polynomial zeros, factoring, multiplicity, sign behavior, the Rational Zero Theorem, and polynomial graphs.

**Estimated Completion:** 6–7 minutes.

## The Scenario

"The trail team has received a polynomial describing another section of the pump track.

The expanded equation is available, but the factored form is missing.

You need to discover where the profile crosses the reference elevation and how it behaves at each crossing."

## Learning Module A — Zeros and Factors

Introduce the scaled version of Flow Line A:

\[
Q(x)=200P(x)
\]

Therefore:

\[
Q(x)=x^4-16x^3+76x^2-96x
\]

Explain that multiplying a polynomial by a nonzero constant changes its vertical scale but not its zeros.

This scaled expression makes the factoring calculations easier while preserving all reference-elevation crossing locations.

Factor out x:

\[
Q(x)=x(x^3-16x^2+76x-96)
\]

The zeros of the original scaled polynomial are still the zeros of P(x).

### Interactive Challenge

Highlight:
- The equation.
- The graph.
- The reference elevation.
- The x-intercepts.

Ask:

"Where does this track profile cross the reference elevation?"

Expected answers:

\[
x=0,\ 2,\ 6,\ 8
\]

Explain that zeros correspond to factors.

For example:

If x = 2 is a zero, then (x - 2) is a factor.

## Learning Module B — Multiplicity

Title:

"Does the Trail Cross the Line or Bounce Off It?"

Introduce the idea of multiplicity.

Explain that the number of times a factor appears determines the multiplicity of its associated zero.

For this lesson, use a second fictional polynomial profile:

\[
M(x)=0.02(x-1)(x-3)^2(x-5)
\]

Domain:

\[
0\leq x\leq6
\]

Its roots are:
- x = 1, multiplicity 1.
- x = 3, multiplicity 2.
- x = 5, multiplicity 1.

Explain:

At an odd-multiplicity zero, the graph crosses the x-axis.

At an even-multiplicity zero, the graph touches the x-axis and turns back without crossing.

### Interactive Component: Multiplicity Explorer

Create an interactive graph showing three root types.

Students can choose:
- Multiplicity 1.
- Multiplicity 2.
- Multiplicity 3.
- Multiplicity 4.

The graph should update to show:
- Crossing behavior.
- Touching behavior.
- Flattening around roots with higher multiplicities.

Use clearly labeled points.

Connect the mathematical features to the fictional reference-elevation profile.

Do not imply that the roots prescribe actual track geometry.

### Challenge

Show the graph of M(x).

Ask students to identify:
- Which roots have odd multiplicity.
- Which roots have even multiplicity.
- Which points cross the reference line.
- Which point touches it and turns back.

## Learning Module C — Build a Polynomial From Its Zeros

Explain:

"If we know the zeros of a polynomial, we can construct its factored form."

For Flow Line A:

\[
x=0,\ 2,\ 6,\ 8
\]

The corresponding factors are:

\[
x,\ (x-2),\ (x-6),\ (x-8)
\]

The function is:

\[
P(x)=a x(x-2)(x-6)(x-8)
\]

The scale factor a determines the vertical scale.

For this example:

\[
a=0.005
\]

Allow students to reconstruct the function from a set of provided zeros.

### Interactive Component: Build the Profile

Display a list of reference-elevation crossings.

Students drag the corresponding factors into a function-building workspace.

As factors are added:
- The equation updates.
- The graph changes.
- New zeros appear.
- The degree updates.
- Maximum possible turning points update.

This should make the relationship between roots, factors, and polynomial degree intuitive.

## Learning Module D — Rational Zero Theorem

Return to:

\[
Q(x)=x^4-16x^3+76x^2-96x
\]

After factoring out x:

\[
x^3-16x^2+76x-96
\]

Introduce the Rational Zero Theorem.

Explain:

"For a polynomial with integer coefficients, possible rational zeros can be identified using factors of the constant term divided by factors of the leading coefficient."

Here:
- Constant term = -96.
- Leading coefficient = 1.

Possible rational zeros are positive and negative integer factors of 96.

Since the actual track segment is defined for:

\[
0\leq x\leq8
\]

Focus on viable nonnegative candidates within this range.

Students should test candidates to identify the actual zeros.

Use:
- Substitution.
- A graph.
- The Remainder Theorem.

Expected candidate zeros:

\[
x=2,\ 6,\ 8
\]

### Interactive Challenge: Find the Hidden Crossing

Give students a polynomial with several possible rational zeros.

Allow them to test a candidate value.

The calculator should display:
- Candidate x-value.
- Calculated polynomial output.
- Whether the output is zero.
- Whether the candidate is an actual zero.

This introduces polynomial root-finding through experimentation.

# 7. MISSION 4 — THE POLYNOMIAL DIAGNOSTICS CENTER

**Mathematical Focus:** Synthetic division, polynomial long division, Remainder Theorem, Factor Theorem, and polynomial reconstruction.

**Estimated Completion:** 5–6 minutes.

## The Scenario

"The pump track profile has been partially analyzed.

One of the feature transitions is known, but the remaining mathematical structure is hidden.

The engineering team needs you to divide the polynomial and uncover the remaining factors."

## Learning Module A — Synthetic Division

Return to the scaled Flow Line polynomial:

\[
Q(x)=x^4-16x^3+76x^2-96x
\]

Suppose the team has already confirmed that x = 2 is one of the zeros.

Use synthetic division to divide by:

\[
x-2
\]

Display coefficients:

\[
1,\ -16,\ 76,\ -96,\ 0
\]

Perform synthetic division:

- Bring down 1.
- Multiply by 2 and add.
- Continue through the coefficients.

Result:

\[
\text{Quotient}=x^3-14x^2+48x
\]

Remainder:

\[
0
\]

Factor the quotient:

\[
x^3-14x^2+48x
\]

\[
=x(x^2-14x+48)
\]

\[
=x(x-6)(x-8)
\]

Therefore:

\[
Q(x)=x(x-2)(x-6)(x-8)
\]

The four zeros have been reconstructed.

### Interactive Component: Synthetic Division Console

Build an interactive synthetic division interface.

Students should be able to:
- Enter polynomial coefficients.
- Select a candidate root.
- Perform each multiplication.
- Add coefficients.
- View the resulting quotient.
- Identify the remainder.

Support:
- Guided mode.
- Independent practice mode.

Give immediate feedback at each step.

Highlight any sign or arithmetic mistakes.

## Learning Module B — Remainder and Factor Theorems

Explain the Remainder Theorem:

"When a polynomial is divided by (x - k), the remainder is f(k)."

Use:

\[
Q(x)=x^4-16x^3+76x^2-96x
\]

Test the value x = 3.

\[
Q(3)=81-432+684-288
\]

\[
Q(3)=45
\]

Therefore, when Q(x) is divided by (x - 3), the remainder is 45.

Since the remainder is not zero, (x - 3) is not a factor of Q(x).

By contrast:

\[
Q(2)=0
\]

Therefore, (x - 2) is a factor.

Connect this to the visual profile:

- A value of zero indicates a crossing of the reference line.
- A nonzero value indicates the profile is above or below the reference line at that point.

Remember that Q is vertically scaled relative to P:

\[
P(3)=0.225\text{ meters}
\]

The zero locations remain unchanged.

### Interactive Component: Test a Feature Location

Let students enter a proposed x-value.

Show:
- The original polynomial.
- The selected candidate.
- The remainder.
- The graph marker.
- Whether the selected value is a zero or not.

Use this as an interactive introduction to the Factor and Remainder Theorems.

## Learning Module C — Polynomial Long Division

Introduce polynomial long division as a broader method for dividing polynomials.

Use:

\[
Q(x)=x^4-16x^3+76x^2-96x
\]

Divide by:

\[
x^2-8x
\]

The result is:

\[
x^2-8x+12
\]

Remainder:

\[
0
\]

Therefore:

\[
Q(x)=(x^2-8x)(x^2-8x+12)
\]

Explain how the division algorithm expresses a polynomial in the form:

\[
f(x)=d(x)q(x)+r(x)
\]

Where:
- d(x) is the divisor.
- q(x) is the quotient.
- r(x) is the remainder.
- The remainder has degree lower than the divisor, or is zero.

### Interactive Component: Polynomial Division Workbench

Build a long division interface.

Display the polynomial division vertically in the familiar mathematical layout.

Allow students to:
- Select the leading term.
- Multiply.
- Subtract.
- Bring down the next term.
- Complete each step.

Show the quotient and remainder.

Explain why each step is performed.

Include a few additional practice problems with integer coefficients.

# 8. OPTIONAL EXTENSION — COMPLEX ROOTS

Create an optional advanced challenge.

Title:

"Some Trail Models Have Roots We Can't See on the Real Graph."

Explain that polynomial functions can have complex roots even when they have no additional real x-intercepts.

Use:

\[
R(x)=(x^2+1)(x-2)(x-6)
\]

The real zeros are:

\[
x=2,\quad x=6
\]

The factor:

\[
x^2+1=0
\]

produces:

\[
x=\pm i
\]

Explain that the graph in the real coordinate plane shows two x-intercepts, even though the polynomial has four complex zeros when counted with multiplicity.

Clarify that complex zeros are algebraic results, not physical locations on a trail.

Introduce the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra and complex-conjugate pairs for polynomials with real coefficients.

Do not force complex values into an artificial physical mountain biking interpretation.

# 9. FINAL MISSION — DESIGN THE PERFECT FLOW LINE

Unlock this mission after the four primary stations are completed.

Title:

# THE FINAL TRACK ANALYSIS

"Your team has been given the polynomial model for a new section of pump track.

You have to identify the features, find its roots, reconstruct the equation, and explain the overall shape.

This time, you're the engineer. The crew is waiting for your analysis."

## The Final Polynomial

Give students:

\[
F(x)=0.002(x-1)(x-4)(x-7)
\]

Domain:

\[
0\leq x\leq8
\]

The function is a fictional profile measured in vertical meters relative to a reference line, with x measured in meters.

## Challenge A — Identify the Polynomial

Ask:
1. What is the degree?
2. What is the leading coefficient?
3. What is the leading term?
4. What is the maximum number of turning points?
5. What is the end behavior?

Expected:
- Degree: 3.
- Leading coefficient: 0.002.
- Leading term: 0.002x³.
- Maximum turning points: 2.
- Left end falls, right end rises in the unrestricted mathematical graph.

Emphasize that the actual modeled track is limited to the defined domain.

## Challenge B — Find the Zeros

The factored form already shows:

\[
x=1,\quad x=4,\quad x=7
\]

Ask students to interpret these as crossings of the reference elevation.

All three roots have multiplicity 1.

Therefore, the graph crosses the reference line at each zero.

## Challenge C — Convert to Standard Form

Expand:

\[
F(x)=0.002(x-1)(x-4)(x-7)
\]

Multiply:

\[
(x-1)(x-4)(x-7)
\]

First:

\[
(x-1)(x-4)=x^2-5x+4
\]

Then:

\[
(x^2-5x+4)(x-7)
\]

\[
=x^3-12x^2+39x-28
\]

Final:

\[
F(x)=0.002x^3-0.024x^2+0.078x-0.056
\]

Ask students to identify its leading coefficient and constant term.

## Challenge D — Test a Candidate Factor

Define:

\[
G(x)=500F(x)
\]

\[
G(x)=x^3-12x^2+39x-28
\]

Ask whether (x - 1) is a factor.

Use synthetic division.

Expected:
- Quotient: \(x^2-11x+28\).
- Remainder: 0.

Factor the quotient:

\[
x^2-11x+28=(x-4)(x-7)
\]

This reconstructs the original three factors.

## Challenge E — Use the Remainder Theorem

Ask:

"What is the value of the polynomial at x = 2?"

\[
G(2)=8-48+78-28
\]

\[
G(2)=10
\]

Therefore:

\[
F(2)=0.02
\]

Explain that the profile is 0.02 meters above the reference line at this modeled position.

This checks whether the student understands that a polynomial can be evaluated even when the input is not a zero.

## Common Misconceptions to Track

Polynomial fundamentals:
- Confusing degree with number of terms.
- Misidentifying the leading coefficient.
- Confusing end behavior for odd and even degrees.
- Assuming the graph can have unlimited turning points.

Polynomial operations:
- Combining terms with different exponents.
- Forgetting signs.
- Incorrect distribution.
- Making errors when multiplying binomials.

Factoring and zeros:
- Confusing zeros with factors.
- Forgetting to set factors equal to zero.
- Confusing simple and repeated roots.
- Misinterpreting crossing and touching behavior.
- Confusing roots with turning points.

Polynomial division:
- Losing coefficients.
- Omitting zero coefficients for missing powers.
- Making sign mistakes during synthetic division.
- Incorrectly calculating the remainder.
- Confusing the quotient and divisor.

The feedback system should identify the likely mathematical misconception and offer a targeted explanation.

Allow students to retry without penalty.

# FINAL PRODUCT PRINCIPLE

The Pump Track Profile Lab should show students that polynomial functions are a way to describe and understand complex mathematical curves.

They begin with a track containing a series of rollers and dips.

They discover its mathematical structure, learn how polynomial operations change the model, uncover the hidden zeros and factors, and use polynomial division to reconstruct the profile.

They should leave understanding that the graph and equation are two ways of describing the same mathematical relationship.

**The goal is to make students feel like they're learning the mathematics behind shaping the flow of a mountain bike track.**

That is the Mathlete MTB experience.
