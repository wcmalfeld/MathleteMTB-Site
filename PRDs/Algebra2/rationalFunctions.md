# 1. PRODUCT VISION

## The Big Idea

Create an interactive mountain bike enduro race analysis experience where students learn rational and radical functions through race timing, trail surveying, and bicycle geometry.

The student becomes an enduro race course analyst.

Their team has been given a fictional race course and needs to analyze the route before race day.

Their responsibilities include:

- Calculate how long it takes riders to complete a timed stage at different average speeds.
- Combine timing information from multiple trail sections.
- Identify mathematical restrictions and discontinuities in route calculations.
- Survey elevation changes and calculate straight-line distances.
- Investigate the geometry of a bicycle wheel using radical functions.
- Solve rational and radical equations.
- Identify and reject extraneous mathematical solutions.

The lesson should feel like a professional race analytics and trail surveying workstation.

## Core Educational Philosophy

Rational functions allow students to understand relationships involving division and changing rates.

Radical functions allow students to understand relationships involving roots, distances, and geometric constraints.

Mountain biking provides authentic settings for both.

The central question:

**How can mathematics help us understand the time, distance, and geometry of a mountain bike course?**

The student should finish with an understanding of how these mathematical relationships work, not simply how to plug numbers into formulas.

# 4. MISSION 1 — BEAT THE CLOCK

**Mathematical Focus:** Rational functions, inverse variation, function transformations, domain, range, and asymptotes.

**Estimated Completion:** 5–6 minutes.

## The Scenario

"Your team is analyzing a 6-kilometer timed enduro stage.

The rider's average speed will determine how long the stage takes.

There's also an 8-minute neutralized transition between the race start area and the timed stage.

Your job is to understand how changing average speed affects the total elapsed time."

Introduce the relationship:

\[
\text{Time}=\frac{\text{Distance}}{\text{Speed}}
\]

Use a fixed course distance of 6 kilometers.

Let:

- v = average riding speed in km/h.
- T(v) = total elapsed time in minutes.

Convert hours to minutes:

\[
T(v)=8+\frac{6}{v}\times60
\]

Simplify:

\[
\boxed{T(v)=8+\frac{360}{v}}
\]

The 8 minutes represent fixed neutralized transition time.

This is an idealized timing model assuming one representative average speed for the timed section.

## Learning Module A — Discover Inverse Variation

Before revealing the equation, ask:

"If a rider completes the same distance at a higher average speed, what happens to their time?"

Allow students to predict.

Then introduce the function.

\[
T(v)=8+\frac{360}{v}
\]

Display:

| Average speed | Timed-stage time | Total elapsed time |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 12 km/h | 30 min | 38 min |
| 18 km/h | 20 min | 28 min |
| 24 km/h | 15 min | 23 min |
| 30 km/h | 12 min | 20 min |

Explain:

"As average speed increases, the amount of time required to cover a fixed distance decreases."

The riding-time component is inversely proportional to speed.

\[
t(v)=\frac{360}{v}
\]

## Interactive Component: Race Clock Simulator

Create an interactive race timing experience.

Visual elements:
- Enduro trail map.
- Rider progressing along the stage.
- Digital stopwatch.
- Speed readout.
- Stage completion indicator.
- Total elapsed time display.

Provide a speed slider:

\[
0<v\leq40
\]

When the student changes speed:
- Stage time updates.
- Total elapsed time updates.
- Rider animation speed changes.
- Timer updates.
- Graph updates.

Display two timing components separately:

1. Fixed transition time: 8 minutes.
2. Variable stage time: 360/v minutes.

This distinction is important because students should understand why the function approaches 8 minutes rather than zero.

### Learning Module B — Domain and Range

Explain:

"The domain represents possible inputs. The range represents possible outputs."

For the mathematical function:

\[
T(v)=8+\frac{360}{v}
\]

The denominator cannot equal zero.

Therefore:

\[
v\neq0
\]

However, speed is positive in this race scenario:

\[
v>0
\]

Therefore, the contextual domain is:

\[
(0,\infty)
\]

The contextual range is:

\[
(8,\infty)
\]

Explain:

- The speed cannot be zero in a completed timed stage.
- The total elapsed time must be greater than 8 minutes because the neutralized transition always takes 8 minutes.
- Increasing speed reduces the variable stage time, but never removes the fixed transition time.

### Learning Module C — Asymptotes

Introduce the two asymptotes.

Vertical asymptote:

\[
v=0
\]

Horizontal asymptote:

\[
T=8
\]

Explain:

As speed approaches zero from the positive side, stage time increases without bound.

As speed grows larger, stage time approaches zero, leaving only the fixed 8-minute transition.

### Interactive Component: Asymptote Explorer

Display the rational function graph.

Allow students to:
- Move a speed marker.
- Observe the changing time.
- Toggle vertical and horizontal asymptotes.
- Compare different route distances.
- Change the fixed transition time.

Use the general model:

\[
T(v)=t_0+\frac{60D}{v}
\]

Where:
- D is route distance in kilometers.
- t0 is fixed transition time in minutes.

Let students change D and t0.

Explain:
- Changing D produces a vertical scaling of the reciprocal component.
- Changing t0 shifts the graph vertically.
- The vertical asymptote remains at v = 0.
- The horizontal asymptote becomes T = t0.

### Independent Challenge

The team wants a total elapsed time of 26 minutes.

How fast must the rider average over the 6-kilometer stage?

Construct:

\[
26=8+\frac{360}{v}
\]

Subtract 8:

\[
18=\frac{360}{v}
\]

Solve:

\[
18v=360
\]

\[
v=20
\]

Answer:

20 km/h.

Ask the student to explain why this is a reasonable input for the model.

# 5. MISSION 2 — THE STAGE TIMING CONSOLE

**Mathematical Focus:** Rational expressions, operations with rational expressions, domain restrictions, rational function graphs, holes, and rational equations.

**Estimated Completion:** 6–7 minutes.

## The Scenario

"The race course contains two different timed sections.

The first section is relatively smooth and flowing.

The second section is more technical, so the rider's average speed is lower.

Your team wants to calculate the total time and compare different pacing strategies."

## Define the Variables

Let:

- v = average speed on the flow section, in km/h.
- The technical section's modeled average speed is v - 5 km/h.

This 5 km/h difference is a fictional modeling assumption used for mathematical practice, not a universal relationship between trail difficulty and speed.

Use:

**Flow section**
- Distance: 2 km.
- Average speed: v km/h.

**Technical section**
- Distance: 1 km.
- Average speed: v - 5 km/h.

**Neutralized transition**
- Fixed time: 8 minutes.

The physical domain requires:

\[
v>5
\]

Both riding speeds must be positive.

## Learning Module A — Construct the Rational Function

Time on the flow section:

\[
F(v)=\frac{120}{v}
\]

Time on the technical section:

\[
S(v)=\frac{60}{v-5}
\]

Total time:

\[
T(v)=8+\frac{120}{v}+\frac{60}{v-5}
\]

Explain how two rational functions can be combined to create a more complex rational function.

### Interactive Component: Split-Time Simulator

Display a trail map divided into two sections.

Show:
- Flow section.
- Technical section.
- Rider speed.
- Separate section timers.
- Total elapsed time.

Allow the student to adjust v.

The technical speed should automatically update to v - 5.

Show both section times and the total.

At v = 15 km/h:

Flow time:

\[
\frac{120}{15}=8\text{ minutes}
\]

Technical time:

\[
\frac{60}{10}=6\text{ minutes}
\]

Total:

\[
8+8+6=22\text{ minutes}
\]

Make the function and the simulation stay synchronized.

## Learning Module B — Add Rational Expressions

Start with:

\[
T(v)=8+\frac{120}{v}+\frac{60}{v-5}
\]

Find a common denominator:

\[
v(v-5)
\]

Combine the fractions:

\[
\frac{120(v-5)+60v}{v(v-5)}
\]

Simplify:

\[
\frac{180v-600}{v(v-5)}
\]

Now include the fixed 8-minute transition:

\[
T(v)=
\frac{8v(v-5)+180v-600}{v(v-5)}
\]

Simplify:

\[
\boxed{
T(v)=\frac{8v^2+140v-600}{v^2-5v}
}
\]

Explain each operation.

Display the original and simplified forms simultaneously.

## Learning Module C — Domain Restrictions and Asymptotes

The algebraic denominator is:

\[
v(v-5)
\]

Therefore:

\[
v\neq0,\quad v\neq5
\]

The mathematical rational function has vertical asymptotes at:

\[
v=0,\quad v=5
\]

The numerator and denominator have the same degree.

The horizontal asymptote is the ratio of their leading coefficients:

\[
T=8
\]

However, the real-world race model requires:

\[
v>5
\]

Therefore, only the graph branch where v is greater than 5 represents a valid race scenario.

Explain the difference between:
- The mathematical domain of the rational expression.
- The contextual domain of the race model.

### Interactive Component: Rational Graph Analyzer

Display the full mathematical graph.

Highlight:
- Vertical asymptotes.
- Horizontal asymptote.
- Domain restrictions.
- Contextual domain.

Allow students to switch between:
1. Full mathematical view.
2. Real-world race view.

Ask students to identify which parts of the graph have valid physical interpretations.

## Learning Module D — Multiplying, Dividing, and Subtracting Rational Expressions

Use the same stage data to introduce the remaining rational-expression operations.

### Subtraction: Compare the Two Section Times

Subtract:

\[
F(v)-S(v)
\]

Substitute:

\[
\frac{120}{v}-\frac{60}{v-5}
\]

Find a common denominator:

\[
\frac{120(v-5)-60v}{v(v-5)}
\]

Simplify:

\[
\boxed{
\frac{60(v-10)}{v(v-5)}
}
\]

At v = 15:

\[
\frac{60(5)}{15(10)}=2
\]

The flow section takes 2 minutes longer than the technical section because the flow section is twice as long, despite its higher average speed.

Ask the student to explain why the result is positive.

### Multiplication: Adjust a Time Estimate

The time required to cover one kilometer at the flow-section speed is:

\[
P(v)=\frac{60}{v}
\]

The ratio between the flow speed and technical speed is:

\[
R(v)=\frac{v}{v-5}
\]

Multiply:

\[
P(v)R(v)=
\frac{60}{v}\times\frac{v}{v-5}
\]

Cancel the common factor:

\[
\boxed{
\frac{60}{v-5}
}
\]

Explain that the result is the time per kilometer at the slower technical-section speed.

This shows how multiplication can combine rational expressions while preserving a meaningful result.

### Division: Compare Section Times

Ask:

"How many times as long does the technical section take compared with the flow section?"

Construct:

\[
\frac{S(v)}{F(v)}
\]

\[
=
\frac{60}{v-5}\div\frac{120}{v}
\]

Multiply by the reciprocal:

\[
\frac{60}{v-5}\times\frac{v}{120}
\]

Simplify:

\[
\boxed{
\frac{v}{2(v-5)}
}
\]

At v = 15:

\[
\frac{15}{2(10)}=0.75
\]

The technical section takes 75% as much time as the flow section.

Emphasize that the two sections have different lengths.

## Learning Module E — Removable Discontinuities

Title:

**"The Missing GPS Reading"**

Introduce a mathematical calibration diagnostic.

The race computer calculates an average change in a squared-speed data value relative to a reference reading of 5 km/h:

\[
C(v)=\frac{v^2-25}{v-5}
\]

Factor the numerator:

\[
C(v)=\frac{(v-5)(v+5)}{v-5}
\]

Simplify:

\[
C(v)=v+5
\]

But the original function is still undefined at:

\[
v=5
\]

Therefore, the graph is a straight line with a hole at:

\[
(5,10)
\]

Explain:

"Even though the expression simplifies, the original function excluded v = 5 because the denominator was zero."

This is a removable discontinuity.

Clearly identify this as a mathematical data-calibration example, not a physical bike performance relationship.

### Interactive Component: Find the Missing Point

Display the simplified line.

Allow the student to:
- Factor the original expression.
- Cancel common terms.
- Identify the excluded input.
- Plot the missing point.
- Distinguish a hole from a vertical asymptote.

## Learning Module F — Solve a Rational Equation

Return to the split-stage timing model.

The team's target is 22 minutes total.

Construct:

\[
8+\frac{120}{v}+\frac{60}{v-5}=22
\]

Subtract 8:

\[
\frac{120}{v}+\frac{60}{v-5}=14
\]

Multiply by the common denominator:

\[
120(v-5)+60v=14v(v-5)
\]

Simplify:

\[
7v^2-125v+300=0
\]

Factor:

\[
(v-15)(7v-20)=0
\]

Candidate solutions:

\[
v=15,\quad v=\frac{20}{7}
\]

The domain requires:

\[
v>5
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{v=15}
\]

The second candidate must be rejected because it would make the technical section's modeled speed negative.

### Independent Challenge

Give students a new two-section timing scenario with different distances and a different speed difference.

Require them to:
1. Define variables.
2. Construct rational expressions.
3. Find a common denominator.
4. Solve the resulting equation.
5. Check the original restrictions.
6. Interpret the valid solution.

Use values that produce manageable calculations.

# 6. MISSION 3 — SURVEY THE LINE

**Mathematical Focus:** Radical functions, square roots, rational exponents, domain and range, Pythagorean relationships, and solving radical equations.

**Estimated Completion:** 5–6 minutes.

## The Scenario

"The trail development team has surveyed two points along a straight section of mountain bike trail.

They know the horizontal distance between the points and the elevation difference.

But they need to calculate the direct distance between the two survey points.

Your job is to help the crew reconstruct the terrain measurement."

Explain the Pythagorean relationship:

\[
a^2+b^2=c^2
\]

Where:
- a is horizontal run.
- b is vertical elevation difference.
- c is straight-line distance.

Clarify that this calculates the straight-line distance between survey points, not the length of a winding trail route.

## Learning Module A — Build a Radical Function

Fix the horizontal run at 120 meters.

Let:

- h = signed elevation difference in meters.
- D(h) = straight-line distance between the survey points.

Construct:

\[
D(h)=\sqrt{120^2+h^2}
\]

Simplify:

\[
\boxed{
D(h)=\sqrt{14400+h^2}
}
\]

This is a radical function.

The domain is:

\[
(-\infty,\infty)
\]

Because h represents signed elevation change, it may be positive for an uphill segment or negative for a downhill segment.

The range is:

\[
[120,\infty)
\]

The direct distance can never be less than the horizontal distance.

### Interactive Component: Trail Survey Simulator

Display a right-triangle visualization.

The triangle should show:
- Horizontal run: 120 meters.
- Vertical elevation difference: adjustable.
- Diagonal straight-line distance.
- Mathematical equation.

Allow the student to manipulate the elevation difference.

Suggested range:

\[
-60\leq h\leq60
\]

When the student changes h:
- The triangle changes.
- The diagonal changes.
- The radical expression updates.
- The graph updates.
- The measurement display updates.

Show both positive and negative elevation differences.

Explain why the diagonal distance remains the same for equal-magnitude uphill and downhill changes.

## Learning Module B — Evaluate and Interpret the Radical Function

Example:

The survey team records an elevation change of 16 meters.

\[
D(16)=\sqrt{14400+16^2}
\]

\[
D(16)=\sqrt{14656}
\]

Simplify:

\[
D(16)=8\sqrt{229}
\]

Approximate:

\[
D(16)\approx121.06\text{ meters}
\]

Show the difference between:
- Exact radical form.
- Decimal approximation.

Explain why the function output represents a distance rather than the horizontal run.

### Trail Grade Connection

Introduce percent grade:

\[
\text{Grade}=
\frac{\text{Elevation change}}{\text{Horizontal run}}\times100
\]

For this example:

\[
\frac{16}{120}\times100
\approx13.33\%
\]

Explain that the grade describes steepness, whereas the radical function calculates the straight-line distance.

These measurements answer different questions.

Present all trail data as a fictional educational surveying exercise, not a trail-construction recommendation.

## Learning Module C — Solve a Radical Equation

The team has a direct measurement of 122 meters.

They know the horizontal run is 120 meters.

What was the elevation difference?

Construct:

\[
\sqrt{14400+h^2}=122
\]

Square both sides:

\[
14400+h^2=14884
\]

\[
h^2=484
\]

\[
h=\pm22
\]

Both solutions are valid because h is a signed elevation difference.

Interpretation:
- +22 meters means the destination is higher.
- -22 meters means the destination is lower.

The measurement alone cannot determine which direction the elevation changes.

This is an excellent opportunity to teach that the sign of a mathematical solution must be interpreted within the problem's context.

## Learning Module D — Verify a Radical Equation

Introduce a fictional survey-console warning.

"The computer reports that the diagonal distance is two meters shorter than the horizontal run."

Use the equation:

\[
\sqrt{r^2+16}=r-2
\]

Where r represents the horizontal run in meters.

Square both sides:

\[
r^2+16=(r-2)^2
\]

Expand:

\[
r^2+16=r^2-4r+4
\]

Simplify:

\[
12=-4r
\]

\[
r=-3
\]

Check the original equation:

\[
\sqrt{(-3)^2+16}=5
\]

But:

\[
-3-2=-5
\]

Therefore:

\[
5\neq-5
\]

The candidate is extraneous.

Explain that the original equation has no physically valid solution.

The student should understand why squaring both sides can create a candidate that does not satisfy the original radical equation.

# 7. MISSION 4 — WHEEL GEOMETRY LAB

**Mathematical Focus:** Square-root functions, radical transformations, rational exponents, domain and range, and radical equations.

**Estimated Completion:** 5–6 minutes.

## The Scenario

"Your team is measuring a mountain bike wheel to understand its circular geometry.

You know the wheel's radius, but you want to understand the vertical position of different points around the wheel.

Mathematical functions can describe the relationship between a point's horizontal position and its vertical position."

## The Mathematical Model

Use an illustrative wheel radius:

\[
R=0.36\text{ meters}
\]

Place the hub at the origin.

The equation of the upper half of a circle is:

\[
x^2+y^2=R^2
\]

Rearrange:

\[
y^2=R^2-x^2
\]

Take the positive square root for the upper half:

\[
\boxed{
W(x)=\sqrt{0.36^2-x^2}
}
\]

Where:
- x is the horizontal offset from the hub.
- W(x) is the vertical coordinate above the hub.

The wheel is idealized as a circle.

## Learning Module A — Understand the Radical Graph

The radicand must be nonnegative:

\[
0.36^2-x^2\geq0
\]

Therefore:

\[
-0.36\leq x\leq0.36
\]

Domain:

\[
[-0.36,0.36]
\]

Range:

\[
[0,0.36]
\]

Explain why the graph does not extend beyond the physical circular wheel.

The mathematical function represents the upper semicircle, not the entire circumference.

### Interactive Component: Wheel Geometry Explorer

Display a large bicycle wheel viewed from the side.

Include:
- Hub center.
- Wheel radius.
- Tire outline.
- Coordinate plane.
- Movable point on the upper semicircle.

Allow the student to move x.

Show:
- Horizontal offset.
- Vertical coordinate.
- Function evaluation.
- Radical expression.
- Wheel radius.

At x = 0:

\[
W(0)=0.36
\]

At x = 0.30:

\[
W(0.30)=\sqrt{0.1296-0.09}
\]

\[
W(0.30)\approx0.199\text{ meters}
\]

Show the point moving along the wheel outline as the student adjusts the input.

## Learning Module B — Solve a Radical Equation

Ask:

"At what horizontal offsets is a point on the upper wheel outline 0.30 meters above the hub?"

Construct:

\[
\sqrt{0.1296-x^2}=0.30
\]

Square both sides:

\[
0.1296-x^2=0.09
\]

\[
x^2=0.0396
\]

\[
x=\pm\sqrt{0.0396}
\]

\[
x\approx\pm0.199
\]

Explain that there are two symmetrical points on the wheel that satisfy the requested vertical height.

Highlight both points.

## Learning Module C — Radical Transformations

Introduce the parent square-root function:

\[
f(x)=\sqrt{x}
\]

Explain transformations using:

\[
g(x)=a\sqrt{x-h}+k
\]

Teach:
- Horizontal shifts.
- Vertical shifts.
- Vertical stretches.
- Vertical compressions.
- Reflections.

Connect this to mathematical profile visualization and the positioning of the wheel within a coordinate system.

### Interactive Component: Radical Profile Studio

Create an adjustable graph with:
- Parent square-root function.
- Transformed function.
- a, h, and k controls.
- Domain and range indicators.

Display the transformation:

\[
g(x)=2\sqrt{x-3}+1
\]

Ask students:
- Where does the graph begin?
- What is the starting output?
- What does the vertical stretch do?
- How does the domain change?

Provide additional examples with negative values of a to show reflections.

Also include the transformed wheel equation:

\[
W(x)=k+\sqrt{R^2-(x-h)^2}
\]

Explain that h and k move the modeled wheel center, while R controls its radius.

This transformation should be presented as a mathematical coordinate model.

Do not present the graph as a tire clearance or frame-fit recommendation.

## Learning Module D — Rational Exponents

Explain:

\[
x^{1/2}=\sqrt{x}
\]

\[
x^{1/3}=\sqrt[3]{x}
\]

\[
x^{1/4}=\sqrt[4]{x}
\]

For the wheel equation:

\[
W(x)=(0.1296-x^2)^{1/2}
\]

Explain how radical notation and rational exponents represent equivalent mathematical expressions.

Include practice converting between:
- Radical notation.
- Fractional exponents.
- Simplified expressions.

### Optional Cube-Root Extension

Introduce a fictional cube-shaped storage container used by the trail crew.

If its volume is V, the length of one side is:

\[
s(V)=\sqrt[3]{V}
\]

For:

\[
V=125\text{ ft}^3
\]

\[
s=\sqrt[3]{125}=5\text{ ft}
\]

Use this as an optional application of cube-root functions.

Make clear that the cube is an idealized storage model.
