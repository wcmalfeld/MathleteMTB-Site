# 1. Lesson Vision

Build an engaging introductory physics lesson that introduces students to the study of the physical world through mountain biking.

The lesson should establish the foundation for everything students will learn throughout Physics of the Ride.

Rather than beginning with equations, definitions, or textbook explanations, students should begin with something familiar: riding a mountain bike.

A mountain biker accelerates down a trail, navigates a berm, jumps over an obstacle, compresses their suspension, and brakes before entering a corner.

Each of these experiences involves physical principles that can be observed, investigated, and explained.

The lesson should help students recognize that physics is not something that only exists in a classroom. It is happening constantly around them.

### Core Lesson Philosophy

**Physics is the science of understanding how the world works. Mountain biking gives us an incredible place to explore it.**

### Central Learning Question

"What can we learn about physics by studying a mountain bike and the way it moves?"

# 2. Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, students should be able to:

1. Define physics and explain what it studies.
2. Identify examples of physics in everyday mountain biking.
3. Understand the difference between matter, energy, motion, and interactions.
4. Identify physical phenomena occurring during a mountain bike ride.
5. Understand what a physical system is and how systems interact with their surroundings.
6. Explain what a scientific model is and why physicists use models.
7. Distinguish between observations, explanations, and scientific predictions.
8. Understand the basic process of scientific investigation.
9. Identify independent, dependent, and controlled variables.
10. Recognize how experiments and evidence help us understand the physical world.
11. Identify major branches of physics and connect them to mountain biking.
12. Appreciate how mathematics provides a language for describing physical phenomena.

### Student Takeaway

By the end of the lesson, students should be able to look at a mountain biker riding down a trail and identify several interesting physical questions worth investigating.

# 3. Lesson Structure

Build the lesson as seven interconnected sections.

# 4. Section 1 — What Is Physics?

## 4.1 Student-Facing Introduction

**Opening question:**

"Have you ever wondered why your mountain bike does the things it does?"

Why does your bike accelerate when you pedal?

Why does it slow down when you stop pedaling?

Why do you lean your bike when going around a corner?

Why does your suspension compress when you land a jump?

Why does a rock feel different when you hit it at 10 km/h compared to 30 km/h?

These questions might seem unrelated, but they have something in common.

They can all be explained using physics.

**Physics is the branch of science that studies matter, energy, motion, forces, and the interactions that explain how the physical world works.**

Physics allows us to understand and predict the behavior of objects and systems, from the smallest particles to enormous galaxies.

And you don't need to look far to find examples.

Your mountain bike is an entire physics laboratory.

## 4.2 What Does Physics Study?

Introduce four foundational ideas.

### A. Matter

Matter is anything that has mass and occupies space.

Examples:
- Your mountain bike.
- Your body.
- The trail.
- Rocks and trees.
- Water and air.

**MTB connection:** Your bike, tires, suspension components, and the terrain are all made of matter with different physical properties.

### B. Energy

Energy is a physical quantity associated with the ability of a system to transfer energy or produce changes.

Examples:
- Kinetic energy.
- Gravitational potential energy.
- Elastic potential energy.
- Thermal energy.
- Electrical energy.

**MTB connection:** A rider at the top of a mountain has gravitational potential energy relative to a lower point on the trail. As they descend, some of that energy can become kinetic energy.

### C. Motion

Motion describes how an object's position changes over time.

Examples:
- A bike accelerating down a trail.
- A wheel rotating.
- A rider jumping through the air.
- A suspension fork compressing and extending.

**MTB connection:** Every time you move down a trail, your position, speed, and direction can change.

### D. Interactions

Objects interact with other objects and their surroundings.

These interactions can involve forces, energy transfer, and changes in motion.

Examples:
- Tires interacting with the ground.
- Brakes interacting with rotating wheels.
- A suspension spring interacting with the bike.
- Gravity interacting with the rider and bike.

**MTB connection:** Your mountain bike cannot ride through a trail independently of the physical world. It constantly interacts with the ground, air, gravity, and its own components.

## 4.3 Interactive Activity — Find the Physics

Display an attractive illustration of a mountain biker on a trail.

Allow students to click on different parts of the scene.

Each selection reveals a question and a short explanation.

| Select | Physics question |
|---|---|
| Front wheel | Why does a rotating wheel keep moving? |
| Tires | How does a tire generate traction? |
| Suspension | How does a spring absorb energy? |
| Brakes | How does braking change motion? |
| Rider | How does mass influence acceleration? |
| Berm | Why does a rider lean into a corner? |
| Jump | Why does a rider follow a curved trajectory? |
| Mountain | How does elevation influence potential energy? |

Each interaction should introduce the concept without giving a full lesson.

The purpose is to create curiosity.

At the end, display:

"Congratulations. You've just identified eight different opportunities to study physics through mountain biking."

# 5. Section 2 — The Physics of a Mountain Bike Ride

This section should help students understand that multiple physics concepts operate simultaneously during a single ride.

## 5.1 Student-Facing Introduction

Imagine starting at the top of a mountain.

You point your bike down the trail and begin riding.

Within just a few seconds, you're experiencing several different physical phenomena.

You accelerate.

Your tires interact with the ground.

You gain speed.

You approach a berm and change direction.

You hit a jump and travel through the air.

You land and compress your suspension.

You brake before entering the next corner.

Every one of these actions involves physics.

Let's break down a ride.

## 5.2 The Seven Stages of a Ride

Create a visual trail sequence with seven clickable stages.

### Stage 1 — Starting at the Top

**What's happening?**

You and your bike are stationary at the top of a mountain.

**Physics concepts:**
- Gravitational potential energy.
- Mass.
- Gravity.
- Equilibrium.

**MTB connection:**

Your position at the top of the mountain gives your rider-bike system gravitational potential energy relative to a lower position.

### Stage 2 — Starting to Move

**What's happening?**

You begin pedaling and move forward.

**Physics concepts:**
- Applied force.
- Newton's laws.
- Acceleration.
- Friction.
- Motion.

**MTB connection:**

Your interaction with the pedals and the tire-ground interaction help produce the net force needed to accelerate the bike.

### Stage 3 — Descending the Trail

**What's happening?**

You gain speed as you travel downhill.

**Physics concepts:**
- Gravitational potential energy.
- Kinetic energy.
- Acceleration.
- Energy transformation.
- Air resistance.

**MTB connection:**

As you descend, gravitational potential energy can be converted into kinetic energy, while some energy is transferred into heat, sound, and other forms.

### Stage 4 — Entering a Berm

**What's happening?**

You lean into a turn and follow a curved path.

**Physics concepts:**
- Circular motion.
- Centripetal acceleration.
- Friction.
- Force components.
- Geometry.

**MTB connection:**

Following a curved path requires inward net acceleration. The forces from the tire-ground interaction help produce the necessary change in direction.

### Stage 5 — Approaching a Jump

**What's happening?**

You compress into the takeoff and launch into the air.

**Physics concepts:**
- Force.
- Acceleration.
- Elastic energy.
- Projectile motion.
- Gravity.

**MTB connection:**

The bike interacts with the takeoff surface, then follows a projectile trajectory while airborne under the simplified projectile-motion model.

### Stage 6 — Landing

**What's happening?**

Your tires contact the landing surface and your suspension compresses.

**Physics concepts:**
- Momentum.
- Impulse.
- Newton's laws.
- Spring forces.
- Energy transfer.

**MTB connection:**

The ground exerts forces on the tires, changing the motion of the rider-bike system, while the suspension stores and transfers energy.

### Stage 7 — Braking

**What's happening?**

You apply the brakes and slow down before the next corner.

**Physics concepts:**
- Friction.
- Force.
- Deceleration.
- Energy transformation.
- Thermal energy.

**MTB connection:**

Braking produces forces that change the bike's motion, while some mechanical energy is transformed into thermal energy within the braking system.

## 5.3 Interactive Activity — One Ride, Seven Physics Lessons

Build an interactive timeline or illustrated trail map.

Students click each of the seven stages.

Display:
- A short animation or illustration.
- The physical phenomenon.
- Two or three relevant physics concepts.
- An MTB-specific explanation.
- A question encouraging further investigation.

After completing the sequence, display all seven stages together.

The purpose is to demonstrate that physics concepts are interconnected.

This activity should become a recurring visual reference throughout Physics of the Ride.

Future lessons can reference the same trail sequence to remind students where they previously encountered the concepts.

# 6. Section 3 — Matter, Energy, Motion, and Interactions

This section expands the four foundational ideas introduced in Section 1.

The goal is to teach students how physicists categorize and investigate the physical world.

## 6.1 Matter

Introduce the idea that physical objects have properties.

Examples:
- Mass.
- Volume.
- Density.
- Shape.
- Material composition.

**MTB connections:**

- A carbon frame and aluminum frame have different material properties.
- A tire has mass and occupies space.
- Air inside a suspension fork is matter.
- Soil and rocks have different physical properties.

Students should understand that the material and physical properties of objects influence how they behave.

## 6.2 Energy

Introduce energy as something that can be transferred and transformed.

Examples:
- Potential energy.
- Kinetic energy.
- Elastic potential energy.
- Thermal energy.
- Chemical energy.

**MTB connections:**

- A rider's body converts chemical energy from food into mechanical work.
- A descending rider converts gravitational potential energy into other forms of energy.
- Suspension springs temporarily store elastic potential energy.
- Braking transforms mechanical energy into thermal energy.

Explain that energy is conserved overall, even when it changes form.

Do not introduce detailed conservation equations yet. Those belong in the Energy and Work modules.

## 6.3 Motion

Introduce the idea of describing how an object moves.

Students should recognize:
- Position.
- Distance.
- Speed.
- Direction.
- Velocity.
- Acceleration.

**MTB connections:**

- A rider traveling down a trail changes position.
- A rider accelerating changes velocity.
- A wheel rotates while the bike moves forward.
- A rider changes direction when cornering.

Detailed calculations belong in the Kinematics module.

## 6.4 Interactions

Introduce the idea that physical objects do not exist independently of their surroundings.

Examples:
- A tire interacts with the trail.
- A brake pad interacts with a rotor.
- A spring interacts with the suspension system.
- A rider interacts with the bike through the handlebars and pedals.
- Gravity acts on the rider-bike system.

**MTB connection:**

When a tire pushes against the ground, the ground also exerts a force on the tire. These interactions are central to understanding traction and propulsion.

This introduces a concept students will revisit in Newton's Third Law.

## 6.5 Interactive Activity — Classify the Physics

Display a collection of mountain biking scenarios.

Students classify each scenario into one or more categories:

- Matter.
- Energy.
- Motion.
- Interaction.

Examples:

**Scenario A:** A rider's suspension fork compresses.

Relevant categories:
- Matter.
- Motion.
- Interaction.
- Energy.

**Scenario B:** A rider travels down a steep hill and gains speed.

Relevant categories:
- Motion.
- Energy.
- Interaction.

**Scenario C:** A tire pushes against the trail.

Relevant categories:
- Matter.
- Interaction.

Allow multiple correct classifications.

The point is that physical phenomena often involve more than one category.

Provide explanations for the selected answers.

# 7. Section 4 — Understanding Physical Systems and Models

This is one of the most important foundations for the rest of the physics course.

Students need to learn how physicists simplify complicated situations to understand them.

## 7.1 What Is a Physical System?

A physical system is the object or collection of objects that we choose to study.

The surroundings are everything outside the chosen system that can interact with it.

For example, we might choose the rider and bike together as our system.

We can then study how the surrounding trail, air, and Earth interact with that system.

The important idea is that the system boundary depends on the question being asked.

## 7.2 Mountain Biking Examples

### Example A — Rider and Bike System

System:
- Rider.
- Mountain bike.

Surroundings:
- Trail.
- Air.
- Earth.

Question:

How does gravity influence the rider-bike system while descending?

### Example B — Suspension System

System:
- Fork.
- Spring.
- Moving suspension components.

Surroundings:
- Rider and bike frame.
- Trail.
- Air.

Question:

How does the suspension system respond when the front wheel hits an obstacle?

### Example C — Tire-Ground System

System:
- Tire.

Surroundings:
- Trail surface.

Question:

How do interactions between the tire and the ground produce traction?

## 7.3 Interactive Activity — Choose Your System

Display three mountain biking scenarios.

For each scenario, students must identify which objects belong to the system being studied.

Example:

"You're studying how a suspension fork compresses when the bike lands from a jump."

Let students select the fork and its relevant components as the system.

Then explain which interactions come from outside the system boundary.

The activity should show that different questions can require different choices of system.

## 7.4 What Is a Scientific Model?

Scientists often use models to represent physical systems.

A model is a simplified representation of a real-world system that helps explain or predict its behavior.

Models can be:
- Physical.
- Mathematical.
- Conceptual.
- Visual.
- Computer-based.

**MTB connections:**

- A trail map is a model of a real trail.
- A free-body diagram represents forces acting on a rider.
- A mathematical equation can predict how a rider accelerates.
- Jump Lab models projectile motion.
- Berm Lab models cornering forces.
- Suspension Lab models spring behavior.

## 7.5 Why Do We Use Models?

The real world is extremely complicated.

A mountain bike contains many moving parts, flexible materials, changing forces, and interactions.

Instead of attempting to calculate everything simultaneously, physicists isolate important variables and make simplifying assumptions.

For example:

Jump Lab initially treats a rider as a projectile after takeoff.

This means we simplify the situation by focusing on horizontal motion and vertical motion under gravity while neglecting effects such as aerodynamic drag and body movement.

The result is a mathematical model that is easier to understand and investigate.

It does not describe every detail of a real jump, but it helps explain important physical relationships.

### Important Teaching Concept

Models are useful because they simplify reality.

However, every model has limitations.

Students should learn to ask:

- What does this model explain?
- What assumptions does it make?
- What does it leave out?
- When might the model become less accurate?

## 7.6 Interactive Activity — Real World vs. Physics Model

Display two versions of the same mountain bike jump.

**REAL-WORLD VERSION**

Show a rider performing a jump with:
- Body movement.
- Aerodynamic drag.
- Suspension movement.
- Rotating wheels.
- Changing bike position.

**SIMPLIFIED PHYSICS MODEL**

Show:
- A projectile trajectory.
- Initial velocity.
- Gravity.
- Horizontal movement.
- Vertical movement.

Ask:

"Which factors did we simplify or leave out to make the mathematical model easier to study?"

Possible selections:
- Aerodynamic drag.
- Rider body movement.
- Suspension dynamics.
- Wheel rotation.

Students should understand that simplifying assumptions are not necessarily mistakes.

They are decisions made to make a problem manageable.

# 8. Section 5 — How Scientists Investigate the World

This section introduces the scientific process.

The objective is to help students understand that physics is not simply a collection of facts.

It is a way of asking questions, testing ideas, and using evidence to understand the world.

## 8.1 Student-Facing Introduction

Imagine you want to find out whether a mountain bike travels faster down a steeper trail.

How would you investigate that?

Would you simply guess?

Would you change the trail angle and measure what happens?

Would you repeat the experiment?

Physicists use systematic investigations to understand relationships between physical quantities.

The process involves asking questions, developing hypotheses, making predictions, testing ideas, analyzing evidence, and improving explanations.

This process is not always perfectly linear. Scientists often revisit earlier steps as they learn more.

## 8.2 The Scientific Investigation Process

Introduce seven stages.

### Step 1 — Ask a Question

Start with something you want to understand.

Example:

"How does trail steepness affect the speed of a mountain bike?"

### Step 2 — Form a Hypothesis

A hypothesis is a proposed explanation or testable idea about what might happen.

Example:

"I think a steeper trail will cause a mountain bike to gain speed more quickly because gravity has a larger component acting downhill."

### Step 3 — Make a Prediction

A prediction describes what you expect to observe if your idea is correct.

Example:

"If I increase the slope angle while keeping the other conditions the same, the bike should experience a greater downhill acceleration in the simplified model."

### Step 4 — Design an Experiment

Decide how to test the question.

Identify:
- What you will change.
- What you will measure.
- What you will keep the same.

### Step 5 — Collect Evidence

Gather observations and measurements.

Example:
- Trail angle.
- Starting speed.
- Speed after a fixed distance.
- Time taken to travel the distance.

### Step 6 — Analyze Results

Look for patterns and relationships.

Does the evidence support your prediction?

Were there unexpected results?

Could measurement uncertainty affect your conclusion?

### Step 7 — Draw a Conclusion

Explain what you discovered and whether the evidence supports your original hypothesis.

Scientists may also revise their model or design a new investigation.

## 8.3 Variables in an Experiment

Introduce three important terms.

**Independent Variable**

The quantity you intentionally change.

**Dependent Variable**

The quantity you measure or observe.

**Controlled Variables**

Other conditions you try to keep the same to make the comparison meaningful.

### MTB Example — Investigating Trail Steepness

Question:

How does trail steepness affect acceleration?

Independent variable:
- Trail angle.

Dependent variable:
- Acceleration.

Controlled variables:
- Rider and bike mass.
- Starting speed.
- Trail surface.
- Distance traveled.
- Other modeled resistance conditions.

Explain that controlling variables helps isolate the effect being investigated.

This introduces experimental thinking before students encounter detailed measurement and uncertainty in Module 3.

## 8.4 Observation vs. Explanation

Introduce the difference between something you observe and the explanation you develop.

Example:

**Observation:**

The mountain bike's suspension compresses when the front wheel hits a bump.

**Explanation:**

The ground exerts a force on the wheel, and the resulting motion causes the suspension system to compress.

**Prediction:**

A different obstacle or riding speed may change the suspension's response.

Students should recognize that observations provide evidence, while scientific explanations use physical principles to interpret that evidence.

## 8.5 Interactive Activity — Design Your Own MTB Experiment

Build an interactive experiment-design activity.

Present the question:

"What happens to a mountain bike's acceleration when the trail gets steeper?"

Students must identify:

1. Independent variable.
2. Dependent variable.
3. Three controlled variables.
4. A hypothesis.
5. A predicted result.

Provide selectable options and a short text input for the hypothesis.

After submission, provide feedback explaining how to design a meaningful experiment.

Allow students to save their investigation.

# 9. Section 6 — The Different Branches of Physics

This section introduces students to the major areas they will explore throughout Physics of the Ride.

Explain that physics is a broad subject with different branches that investigate different physical phenomena.

Every branch should have a relevant mountain biking connection.

## 9.1 Mechanics

Mechanics studies motion, forces, and the behavior of physical objects.

**MTB applications:**
- Acceleration down a trail.
- Newton's laws.
- Jump trajectories.
- Cornering.
- Momentum.
- Suspension.

**Related Mathlete Labs:**
- Trail Speed Lab.
- Jump Lab.
- Berm Lab.
- MTB Force Lab.
- MTB Impact Lab.

## 9.2 Energy and Thermodynamics

Energy physics explores energy, work, power, heat, and the transfer of energy.

**MTB applications:**
- Descending a mountain.
- Climbing.
- Braking.
- Suspension energy.
- Thermal energy generated by friction.

**Related Mathlete Labs:**
- Trail Energy Lab.
- MTB Power Lab.
- Brake Heat Lab.

## 9.3 Rotational Mechanics

Rotational mechanics studies objects that rotate and the forces and torques involved.

**MTB applications:**
- Wheels.
- Cranks.
- Chainrings.
- Cassettes.
- Brake rotors.
- Drivetrain mechanics.

**Related Mathlete Lab:**
- Wheel & Gear Lab.

## 9.4 Fluid Mechanics

Fluid mechanics investigates liquids and gases, including their pressure and flow.

**MTB applications:**
- Hydraulic brakes.
- Suspension oil.
- Air springs.
- Tire pressure.
- Air resistance.

**Related Mathlete Lab:**
- Hydraulic Brake Lab.

## 9.5 Waves and Oscillations

This branch investigates repeated motion, wave behavior, and vibration.

**MTB applications:**
- Suspension oscillations.
- Vibrations transmitted through the bike.
- Mechanical resonance.
- Sound generated by a moving bike.

**Related Mathlete Labs:**
- Suspension Lab.
- Vibration & Wave Lab.

## 9.6 Electricity and Magnetism

This area studies electric charges, electrical circuits, magnetic fields, and electromagnetism.

**MTB applications:**
- E-bike motors.
- Batteries.
- Electric bike sensors.
- Bike lights.
- Wireless cycling computers.

**Related Mathlete Labs:**
- E-Bike Battery Lab.
- Circuit Lab.
- Magnetic Motor Lab.
- Generator Lab.

## 9.7 Optics

Optics studies light and how it interacts with materials and optical systems.

**MTB applications:**
- Protective eyewear.
- Bike lights.
- Reflectors.
- Lenses.
- Cameras.

**Related Mathlete Labs:**
- Light & Lens Lab.
- Vision Lab.
- Light Wave Lab.

# 10. Section 7 — Knowledge Check and Final Challenge

The assessment should test whether students understand what physics studies and how scientists investigate physical phenomena.

Avoid testing detailed equations at this stage.

The objective is conceptual understanding and curiosity.

## 10.1 Knowledge Check

### Question 1 — What Is Physics?

Which statement best describes physics?

A. The study of living organisms.

B. The study of matter, energy, motion, and interactions.

C. The study of historical events.

D. The study of chemical reactions only.

**Correct answer: B.**

Explanation:

Physics helps us understand how the physical world behaves, from moving objects to energy and interactions.

### Question 2 — Physics in Mountain Biking

Which of the following is an example of physics?

A. A bike accelerating downhill.

B. A rider choosing a favorite trail.

C. A rider choosing a helmet color.

D. A rider deciding where to meet friends.

**Correct answer: A.**

Explanation:

Acceleration is a physical phenomenon involving changes in velocity.

### Question 3 — Energy

A rider travels from the top of a mountain toward a lower point.

Which statement best describes the energy involved?

A. Energy disappears as the rider descends.

B. Gravitational potential energy can be transformed into kinetic energy and other forms.

C. The rider creates energy from nothing.

D. Gravity stops acting once the bike moves.

**Correct answer: B.**

### Question 4 — Physical Systems

A physicist wants to study how a suspension fork compresses.

What is an example of a possible system?

A. The entire mountain range.

B. The suspension fork and its relevant components.

C. The weather forecast.

D. A different bicycle on another trail.

**Correct answer: B.**

Explanation:

A physical system is the object or collection of objects selected for investigation.

### Question 5 — Scientific Models

Why do physicists use models?

A. To make every calculation unnecessarily complicated.

B. To represent and investigate real-world systems using simplified descriptions.

C. To avoid making observations.

D. To guarantee that every prediction is perfectly accurate.

**Correct answer: B.**

### Question 6 — Scientific Investigation

You want to discover how slope steepness affects mountain bike acceleration.

What is the independent variable?

A. Rider mass.

B. Trail angle.

C. Acceleration.

D. Starting speed.

**Correct answer: B.**

### Question 7 — Observations

You observe a suspension fork compressing when a bike lands.

Which statement is an observation rather than an explanation?

A. The suspension spring stores elastic potential energy.

B. The landing forces cause suspension compression.

C. The fork becomes shorter as it compresses.

D. The suspension responds because of Newton's laws.

**Correct answer: C.**

### Question 8 — Branches of Physics

Which branch of physics helps explain how hydraulic mountain bike brakes work?

A. Optics.

B. Fluid mechanics.

C. Astronomy.

D. Wave optics.

**Correct answer: B.**

# 11. Final Challenge — Become a Physics Detective

## Challenge Title: Investigate Your Ride

Present students with this scenario:

"You are riding a mountain bike down a trail. You accelerate down a slope, carve through a berm, launch off a jump, land, and brake before the next corner.

Your mission is to identify the physics happening throughout your ride."

Display a trail illustration with five interactive sections:

1. Descending.
2. Accelerating.
3. Cornering.
4. Jumping.
5. Braking.

Students must identify at least one physics concept associated with each stage.

Then ask them to complete the following:

**Part A — Identify**

Name three physical phenomena occurring during your ride.

**Part B — Investigate**

Choose one phenomenon and create a scientific question about it.

**Part C — Predict**

Make a prediction about what would happen if you changed one variable.

**Part D — Explain**

Identify the physics concepts that could help explain your prediction.

**Part E — Reflect**

Why might learning physics help you better understand mountain biking?

Accept short written responses.

Provide supportive feedback that rewards logical thinking rather than requiring advanced physics vocabulary.

Save the completed investigation to the student's lesson progress.
